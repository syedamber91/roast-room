# Stories: The Intelligent Investor (Graham, with Zweig's commentary)

Built from 2,291 verified items in the private store (roast-room-private/intelligent-investor). These are the case histories the book reuses. All company figures are historical: Graham's are mostly US companies of the 1930s to early 1970s, Zweig's are US companies around 1999 to 2002. None is today's data. No call to act is carried over; only the lesson is.

## A. Graham's four case histories (Chapter 17)

### 1. Penn Central: the warning signs were printed
- **Lesson.** Check interest coverage before anything else. Penn Central's interest was earned 1.91 times in 1967 and 1.98 times in 1968, far below the 5 times before tax that Graham's textbook asks of railroad bonds (S017). It went bankrupt in June 1970.
- **Quote.** "Moral: Security analysts should do their elementary jobs before they study stock-market movements, gaze into crystal balls, make elaborate mathematical calculations, or go on all-expense-paid field trips." (S017) [full: S017#warning-4]
- **Ids.** [full: S017#example-1], [full: S017#rule-1], [full: S011#example-1], [full: S015#example-3]. Historical, 1960s US.

### 2. Ling-Temco-Vought: empire building on borrowed money
- **Lesson.** Growth by acquisition, fed by bank credit, can collapse. Leading banks offered LTV at $111; in under three years it was 7 1/8 (S017).
- **Quote.** "An extreme example of quick and unsound" (S017) [full: S017#warning-18]
- **Ids.** [full: S017#example-2], [full: S017#rule-4]. Historical, late 1960s US.

### 3. NVF and Sharon Steel: the small company that swallowed a big one
- **Lesson.** Debt-financed takeovers dressed up by accounting devices. NVF bought a firm about seven times its size with bonds and warrants (S017).
- **Quote.** "a small company absorbed another seven times its size, incurring a huge debt and employing some startling accounting devices" (S017) [full: S017#warning-19]
- **Ids.** [full: S017#example-3], [full: S017#warning-12], [full: X-appendixes#example-22]. Historical, 1969 US.

### 4. AAA Enterprises: a hot issue with nothing underneath
- **Lesson.** A new issue can lose all its capital within months while the market still prices it as alive (S017).
- **Quote.** "There was something sadly, badly, and accusingly wrong about the September 30 report." (S017) [full: S017#warning-14]
- **Ids.** [full: S017#example-4], [full: S017#warning-15]. Historical, 1969 US.

## B. Graham's paired companies (Chapter 18)

### 5. Air Products versus Air Reduction
- **Lesson.** The cheaper, less admired company held up better. Air Products sold at 16 1/2 times earnings against 9.1 for Air Reduction, which traded at 75 percent of book (S018). Air Reduction made the better comeback in early 1971.
- **Ids.** [full: S018#rule-2], [full: S018#example-1]. Historical, 1969 to 1971 US.

### 6. International Flavors and Fragrances versus International Harvester
- **Lesson.** Profitability and growth earn a high multiple, and a high multiple carries its own risk. Flavors sold at 55 times earnings and Harvester at 10.7 times, 41 percent below net worth (S018).
- **Quote.** "The answer lies in the two magic words: profitability and growth." (S018) [full: S018#takeaway-4]
- **Ids.** [full: S018#example-2], [full: S018#example-4], [full: S018#takeaway-6]. Historical, 1969 US.

### 7. McGraw-Hill versus McGraw-Edison
- **Lesson.** Enthusiasm for an industry can double the multiple. McGraw-Hill's multiplier was more than twice McGraw-Edison's on smaller sales and earnings (S018).
- **Quote.** "the speculative hazards in such stocks created by Wall Street through its undisciplined waves of optimism and pessimism" (S018) [full: S018#warning-13]
- **Ids.** [full: S018#example-5], [full: S018#example-6], [full: S018#example-13]. Historical, 1968 to 1970 US.

### 8. National General versus National Presto
- **Lesson.** A conglomerate's size is not value. After counting its warrants, National General's true multiple was 69 times 1968 earnings; it later fell from 60 to 9 (S018).
- **Quote.** "full of corporate gadgets and grandiose gestures, but lacking in substantial values behind the market quotations" (S018) [full: S018#warning-14]
- **Ids.** [full: S018#example-8], [full: S018#example-9], [full: S018#takeaway-7]. Historical, 1968 to 1970 US.

### 9. Whiting versus Willcox & Gibbs, and Realty Equities
- **Lesson.** The market sometimes pays more for the weaker company. Whiting had higher sales, earnings and book value yet a fraction of Willcox & Gibbs' market value (S018). Realty Equities blew its assets and debts up together and ended with an asset value under a tenth of its high price.
- **Ids.** [full: S018#example-10], [full: S018#example-11], [full: S018#warning-1], [full: S018#warning-4]. Historical, 1960s US.

## C. Other Graham cases

### 10. Four companies compared (Chapter 13)
- **Lesson.** ELTRA and Emhart (low multiples) against Emerson Electric and Emery Air Freight (high multiples). Prices varied far more than performance (S013).
- **Quote.** "The most striking fact about the four companies is that the current price/earnings ratios vary much more widely than their operating performance or financial condition." (S013) [full: S013#takeaway-2]
- **Ids.** [full: S013#example-1], [full: S013#warning-3], [full: S013#takeaway-4]. Historical, 1970 US.

### 11. A&P in 1938: below its own working capital
- **Lesson.** Fear can price a sound company below its net current assets. A&P's preferred and common together sold for $126 million against $134 million of working capital (S008).
- **Ids.** [full: S008#example-5], [full: S008#example-6]. Historical, 1938 to 1972 US.

### 12. Xerox versus General Motors: what the price implies
- **Lesson.** A price carries an implied growth rate. The market implied 32.4 percent a year for Xerox and 2.8 percent for GM (S011).
- **Ids.** [full: S011#example-4], [full: S011#example-13]. Historical, 1963 to 1969 US.

### 13. IPO waves and Aetna Maintenance
- **Lesson.** New-issue booms repeat. More than 850 companies listed from June 1960 to May 1962; Aetna Maintenance went from $9 to $15 to 7/8 (S006).
- **Quote.** "In many cases the new issues lose 75% and more of their offering price." (S006) [full: S006#warning-19]
- **Ids.** [full: S006#example-4], [full: S006#example-7], [full: X-appendixes#example-20]. Historical, 1960 to 1964 US.

### 14. GEICO and Graham-Newman
- **Lesson.** A bought-at-a-moderate-price stake can be the one that matters most. Graham and Newman bought half of GEICO for about a fifth of their fund (X-postscript); the postscript draws the diversification lesson from it.
- **Ids.** [full: X-postscript#example-1], [full: S009#example-2], [full: X-postscript#warning-2]. Historical, 1948 onward US.

## D. Zweig's dot-com era cases (around 1999 to 2002)

### 15. Exodus versus Emerson Electric
- **Lesson.** Excitement against reliability. Exodus rose 1,005.8 percent in 1999 while losing money, then filed for Chapter 11 in 2001; Emerson ended 2002 less than 4 percent below its 1999 close (C013).
- **Quote.** "Like its power tools, Emerson was never flashy, but it was reliable" (C013) [full: C013#takeaway-1]
- **Ids.** [full: C013#example-2], [full: C013#example-3], [full: C013#example-5].

### 16. Zweig's pairs: Yahoo! and Yum!, Cisco and Sysco, Commerce One and Capital One
- **Lesson.** Zweig updates Graham's Chapter 18 with bubble pairs. Yahoo! traded at 263 times revenues and 3,264 times earnings; from 2000 through 2002 Yum! rose 25.4 percent while Yahoo! lost 92.4 percent (C018). Commerce One lost 99.7 percent; Capital One lost 38 percent.
- **Quote.** "there is no such thing as a good stock; there are only good stock prices, which come and go." (C018) [full: C018#takeaway-1]
- **Ids.** [full: C018#example-2], [full: C018#example-4], [full: C018#example-1], [full: C018#example-6].

### 17. Tyco, Lucent, AOL Time Warner and eToys
- **Lesson.** Zweig's update of Chapter 17. Tyco's supposedly nonrecurring charges showed up in four of five years; eToys spent $2 to sell every dollar of toys and filed for bankruptcy in 2001 (C017).
- **Ids.** [full: C017#example-2], [full: C017#example-3], [full: C017#example-1], [full: C017#example-4], [full: C017#example-5], [full: C017#takeaway-2].

### 18. VA Linux, Nortel and JDS Uniphase: the cost of overpaying
- **Lesson.** A huge first loss takes decades to recover. VA Linux went from $239.50 to $1.19 in three years (C006); Nortel traded at 87 times guessed earnings before falling from $113.50 to $1.65 (C007).
- **Quote.** "Even at a robust 10% annual rate of return, it will take more than 43 years to break even on this overpriced purchase!" (C020) [full: C020#example-2]
- **Ids.** [full: C006#example-1], [full: C007#example-3], [full: C020#example-1].

### 19. Newton and the South Sea Company
- **Lesson.** Intelligence is not temperament. Newton took a 100 percent profit, went back in at a higher price and lost £20,000 (X-introduction, Zweig's note).
- **Quote.** "By letting the roar of the crowd override his own judgment, the world's greatest scientist acted like a fool." (X-introduction) [full: X-introduction#warning-14]
- **Ids.** [full: X-introduction#example-3].

### 20. Comverse: a net-net in 2002
- **Lesson.** Graham's net-current-asset bargain still turns up after a crash. Comverse had $1.4 billion of net working capital and a market value just under that (C007).
- **Ids.** [full: C007#example-5], [full: S015#example-11].
