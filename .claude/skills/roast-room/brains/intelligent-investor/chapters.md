## Chapter 1: Investing is not speculating

**In one line:** define an investment, then pick a role (defensive or enterprising) and a stock-bond split.

Graham says:
- The definition: "An investment operation is one which, upon thorough analysis promises safety of principal and an adequate return. Operations not meeting these requirements are speculative." (S001) [full: S001#framework-1].
- Three kinds of unintelligent speculation: "the foremost are: (1) speculating when you think you are investing; (2) speculating seriously instead of as a pastime, when you lack proper knowledge and skill for it; and (3) risking more money in speculation than you can afford to lose." (S001) [full: S001#warning-3].
- The defensive investor keeps bonds between 25% and 75%, with stocks the converse [full: S001#rule-1]; the simplest choice is a 50-50 split, restored when it drifts by about 5% [full: S001#method-1].
- The defensive investor sticks to "The defensive investor must confine himself to the shares of important companies with a long record of profitable operations and in strong financial condition." (S001) [full: S001#rule-6].
- The enterprising investor needs extra return worth the effort: about 5% a year before taxes [full: S001#rule-9], and the investor must follow "policies which are (1) inherently sound and promising, and (2) not popular on Wall Street." (S001) [full: S001#rule-8]
- New offerings and hot issues will not beat the average [full: S001#warning-11]; trading is not investing [full: S001#warning-13].

Zweig's commentary adds:
- Graham's test, restated: "Graham urges you to invest only if you would be comfortable owning a stock even if you had no way of knowing its daily share price." (C001) [full: C001#rule-4].
- Put no more than 10% of your wealth at speculative risk "For most of us, 10% of our overall wealth is the maximum permissible amount to put at speculative risk." (C001) [full: C001#rule-7].
- Mechanical formulas stop working once popular [full: C001#warning-12]; Zweig updates this to backtests and data mining [full: C001#warning-11].
- "You must never delude yourself into thinking that you’re investing when you’re speculating." (C001) [full: C001#rule-11]

Tests in this chapter (Graham, 1972): stocks between 25% and 75% [full: S001#rule-5]; rebalance at about 5% drift [full: S001#rule-15]; enterprising target about 5% before taxes [full: S001#rule-9]. Zweig (2003): speculation capped at 10% of wealth [full: C001#rule-7].

Use this chapter when: someone cannot say whether they are investing or speculating, or has no stock-bond split.

## Chapter 2: Inflation

**In one line:** inflation is a reason to own some stocks, not proof that stocks always protect.

Graham says:
- The planning baseline was a 2.5% average yearly price rise [full: S002#rule-1].
- Stocks are no sure hedge: "There is no close time connection between inflationary (or deflationary) conditions and the movement of common-stock earnings and prices." (S002) [full: S002#rule-3].
- Yet Graham holds that "the defensive investor cannot afford to be without an appreciable proportion of common stocks in his portfolio" (S002) [full: S002#rule-8], "even if we regard them as the lesser of two evils" (S002) [full: S002#takeaway-6].
- Other hedges are shaky: collectibles have "seems to be an element of the artificial or the precarious or even the unreal about the quoted prices." (S002) [full: S002#warning-8]; and "Unfortunately, real-estate values are also subject to wide fluctuations" (S002) [full: S002#warning-9].
- Borrowing to own stocks is the danger case: "borrowing more than a third of their net worth to sink 135% of their assets into stocks" (S002) [full: S002#rule-14].

Zweig's commentary adds:
- Judge results after inflation: "it's so important to measure your investing success not just by what you make, but by how much you keep after inflation." (C002) [full: C002#rule-1].
- Stocks have done badly when inflation runs high: "The stock market lost money in eight of the 14 years in which inflation exceeded 6%; the average return for those 14 years was a measly 2.6%." (C002) [full: C002#warning-5].
- Keep "most investors should keep at least a small portion of their assets in bonds, as a form of insurance against deflating prices" (C002) [full: C002#rule-2].
- Zweig's update is inflation-linked government bonds (TIPS), at least 10% of retirement assets [full: C002#rule-4].

Tests in this chapter: 2.5% inflation baseline (Graham, 1972) [full: S002#rule-1]; at least 10% of retirement assets in TIPS (Zweig, 2003) [full: C002#rule-4].

Use this chapter when: someone argues for 100% stocks because of inflation, or for 100% bonds because stocks feel risky.

## Chapter 3: A century of market history

**In one line:** read the market level against earnings and bond yields, and lean to caution when unsure.

Graham says:
- Past gains breed "a quite illogical and dangerous conviction that equally marvelous results could be expected for common stocks in the future" (S003) [full: S003#warning-1].
- Compare stock earnings yields with bond yields; the ratio "the ratio of stock returns (earnings/price) to bond returns has grown worse during the entire period" (S003) [full: S003#framework-5].
- His 1972 policy at a high market: no borrowing "No borrowing to buy or hold securities." (S003) [full: S003#rule-2], and stocks cut to at most 50% [full: S003#rule-4].
- "We suggest, however, that if the investor is in doubt as to which course to pursue he should choose the path of caution." (S003) [full: S003#takeaway-5]

Zweig's commentary adds:
- "The heart of Graham's argument is that the intelligent investor must never forecast the future exclusively by extrapolating the past." (C003) [full: C003#warning-1]
- Zweig builds a return estimate from dividend yield, growth and inflation and gets roughly 6% a year [full: C003#rule-2].
- Shiller's method: "Shiller compares the current price of the Standard & Poor’s 500-stock index against average corporate profits over the past 10 years (after inflation)." (C003) [full: C003#method-1].
- "Staying humble about your forecasting powers, as Graham did, will keep you from risking too much on a view of the future that may well turn out to be wrong." (C003) [full: C003#rule-6]

Tests in this chapter: no borrowing (Graham) [full: S003#rule-2]; stocks at most 50% at the 1972 level [full: S003#rule-4]; about 6% nominal long-run return (Zweig, 2003) [full: C003#rule-2].

Use this chapter when: someone extrapolates recent returns or asks what return to expect.

## Chapter 4: Portfolio policy for the defensive investor

**In one line:** set return goals by effort, keep a fixed split, and avoid weak fixed-income forms.

Graham says:
- "The rate of return sought should be dependent, rather, on the amount of intelligent effort the investor is willing and able to bring to bear on his task." (S004) [full: S004#framework-1]
- At 1972 prices, no more than 50% in stocks "we would counsel against a greater than 50% apportionment to common stocks at this time" (S004) [full: S004#rule-2].
- The 50-50 plan keeps an equal split [full: S004#rule-10]; the item records rebalancing at 55% and 45% as the limit.
- Contrarian maxims are easy to state and hard to follow, because they go against human nature [full: S004#takeaway-1].
- Avoid high-yield bonds: "the ordinary investor is wiser to keep away from such high-yield bonds." (S004) [full: S004#warning-5].
- Preferred stocks: "Really good preferred stocks can and do exist, but they are good in spite of their investment form, which is an inherently bad one." (S004) [full: S004#warning-7] So "they should be bought on a bargain basis or not at all." (S004) [full: S004#rule-9].

Zweig's commentary adds:
- The choice of role is about yourself: financial risk lies within the investor, not only in the economy or the holdings [full: C004#warning-1].
- Graham never ties allocation to age [full: C004#takeaway-1]; Zweig drops the age rule of thumb [full: C004#warning-2].
- Keep the split fixed: "Once you set these target percentages, change them only as your life circumstances change. Do not buy more stocks because the stock market has gone up; do not sell them because it has gone down." (C004) [full: C004#rule-4]. Rebalance every six months [full: C004#method-2].
- Intermediate bonds of 5 to 10 years suit most investors [full: C004#rule-6]; never own a stock for its dividend alone [full: C004#rule-10].

Tests in this chapter: stocks 25% to 75% [full: S004#rule-1], at most 50% in 1972 [full: S004#rule-2]; preferreds only on a bargain basis [full: S004#rule-9]. Zweig (2003): bonds at least 25% [full: C004#rule-2]; rebalance twice a year [full: C004#method-2].

Use this chapter when: setting or defending an asset split, or judging a bond, preferred or high-yield product.

## Chapter 5: The defensive investor and common stocks

**In one line:** four rules for picking stocks, plus regular monthly buying.

Graham says:
- Diversify: "There should be adequate though not excessive diversification. This might mean a minimum of ten different issues and a maximum of about thirty." (S005) [full: S005#rule-1].
- "Each company selected should be large, prominent, and conservatively financed." (S005) [full: S005#rule-2]
- "Each company should have a long record of continuous dividend payments." (S005) [full: S005#rule-12]
- Price limit: "We suggest that this limit be set at 25 times such average earnings, and not more than 20 times those of the last twelve-month period." (S005) [full: S005#rule-10].
- Graham regards growth stocks "as too uncertain and risky a vehicle for the defensive investor" (S005) [full: S005#warning-4].
- Dollar-cost averaging: "an investor devotes the same dollar amount each month to buying one or more common stocks." (S005) [full: S005#method-2].
- Real risk is business loss: "the risk attached to an ordinary commercial business is measured by the chance of its losing money, not by what would happen if the owner were forced to sell." (S005) [full: S005#rule-5].

Zweig's commentary adds:
- Graham's 10 to 30 stocks "remains a good starting point for investors who want to pick their own stocks, but you must make sure that you are not overexposed to one industry." (C005) [full: C005#rule-1]
- Owning your employer's stock in a retirement plan is a trap [full: C005#warning-3].
- Index funds are the ideal vehicle for monthly buying "The ideal way to dollar-cost average is into a portfolio of index funds, which own every stock or bond worth having" (C005) [full: C005#rule-3].
- The defensive investor wins by sitting still [full: C005#framework-1].
- If you trade more than twice a year or spend more than an hour or two a month, something is wrong [full: C005#rule-2].

Tests in this chapter (Graham, 1972): 10 to 30 issues [full: S005#rule-1]; price at most 25 times seven-year average earnings and 20 times the last twelve months [full: S005#rule-10]; industrials conservatively capitalised at about 50% stock equity [full: S005#rule-6]; dividends for at least 10 years [full: S005#rule-18].

Use this chapter when: a cautious investor wants a stock list they can keep without much work.

## Chapter 6: What the enterprising investor should avoid

**In one line:** the "don'ts" for the active investor: second-grade bonds at full price, foreign bonds, and new issues.

Graham says:
- Do not pay around 100 for second-grade bonds "it is mere common sense to abstain from buying securities at around 100 if long experience indicates that they can probably be bought at 70 or less" (S006) [full: S006#rule-4]; the item records 30% under par as the line [full: S006#rule-13].
- Foreign government bonds lack enforcement [full: S006#warning-5].
- Be wary of new issues "He will also be wary of all kinds of new issues, including convertible bonds and preferreds that seem quite tempting" (S006) [full: S006#warning-1]; better to stay out entirely [full: S006#rule-8].
- A sign of a bull market top: "One fairly dependable sign of the approaching end of a bull swing is the fact that new common stocks of small and nondescript companies are offered at prices somewhat higher than the current level for many medium-sized companies with a" (S006) [full: S006#rule-7]
- "an intelligent investor will not forget what happened in 1962 and will let others make the next batch of quick profits in this area and experience the consequent harrowing losses." (S006) [full: S006#takeaway-3]

Zweig's commentary adds:
- On yield chasing: "Buying a bond only for its yield is like getting married only for the sex." (C006) [full: C006#warning-1].
- Zweig caps emerging-market bonds at 10% of a bond portfolio [full: C006#rule-3].
- Day trading is "one of the best weapons ever invented for committing financial suicide." (C006) [full: C006#warning-4]
- IPOs held three years trailed the market by more than 23 points a year, 1980 to 2001 [full: C006#warning-10].
- "A long-term investor is the only kind of investor there is." (C006) [full: C006#framework-1]

Tests in this chapter: second-grade issues only at 70 or less [full: S006#rule-4]; railroad bond coverage 5 times [full: S006#rule-3] (Graham, 1972). Zweig (2003): emerging-market bonds at most 10% [full: C006#rule-3]; turnover of 0.2% a month for the most patient traders [full: C006#rule-6].

Use this chapter when: someone is tempted by an IPO, a high yield, or frequent trading.

## Chapter 7: What the enterprising investor can do

**In one line:** three bargain hunting grounds: unpopular large companies, bargain issues, and special situations.

Graham says:
- Two tests for any active policy, (1) "It must meet objective or rational tests of underlying soundness; and (2) it must be different from the policy followed by most investors or speculators." (S007) [full: S007#framework-1].
- Growth stocks: "Unusually rapid growth cannot keep up forever; when a company has already registered a brilliant expansion, its very increase in size makes a repetition of its achievement more difficult." (S007) [full: S007#warning-4]. He would "advise against the usual type of growth-stock commitment for the enterprising investor" (S007) [full: S007#warning-5].
- "The key requirement here is that the enterprising investor concentrate on the larger companies that are going through a period of unpopularity." (S007) [full: S007#framework-2]
- An issue is not a true bargain "unless the indicated value is at least 50% more than the price." (S007) [full: S007#rule-5] He also asks for no year of earnings deficit over the past decade or more [full: S007#rule-6].
- The clearest bargain sells below net working capital "The type of bargain issue that can be most readily identified is a common stock that sells for less than the company's net working capital alone, after deducting all prior obligations." (S007) [full: S007#rule-7].
- Secondary companies only at "prices not more than two-thirds of the appraisal value of the securities." (S007) [full: S007#rule-14].
- An investor cannot be half a businessman and expect half the normal business profit [full: S007#warning-32].

Zweig's commentary adds:
- "In the financial markets, hindsight is forever 20/20, but foresight is legally blind." (C007) [full: C007#warning-1]
- Growth stocks at a P/E much above 25 or 30 face ugly odds [full: C007#rule-1].
- Temporary unpopularity "unpopularity can create lasting wealth by enabling you to buy a great company at a good price." (C007) [full: C007#takeaway-3].
- Zweig suggests up to a third of stock money in foreign stock funds [full: C007#rule-3].

Tests in this chapter (Graham, 1972): value at least 50% above price [full: S007#rule-5]; no deficit year in a decade or more [full: S007#rule-6]; price below net working capital [full: S007#rule-7]; secondary issues at two-thirds of appraisal or less [full: S007#rule-14]; diversification needs about $100,000 [full: S007#rule-23].

Use this chapter when: an active investor wants a method that is both sound and unpopular.

## Chapter 8: The investor and market fluctuations

**In one line:** price swings are a chance to act on value, never a signal by themselves.

Graham says:
- "There are two possible ways by which he may try to do this: the way of timing and the way of pricing." (S008) [full: S008#framework-1]
- "We are convinced that the intelligent investor can derive satisfactory results from pricing of either type. We are equally sure that if he places his emphasis on timing, in the sense of forecasting, he will end up as a speculator" (S008) [full: S008#takeaway-1]
- "Basically, price fluctuations have only one significant meaning for the true investor. They provide him with an opportunity to buy wisely when prices fall sharply and to sell wisely when they advance a great deal." (S008) [full: S008#rule-8]
- A share has two sides: "The holder of marketable shares actually has a double status, and with it the privilege of taking advantage of either at his choice." (S008) [full: S008#framework-4].
- The cautious investor can stay close to tangible-asset value, say "at not more than one-third above that figure." (S008) [full: S008#rule-4]
- "Good managements produce a good average market price, and bad managements produce bad market prices." (S008) [full: S008#rule-13]
- Long bonds swing with rates too [full: S008#warning-15].

Zweig's commentary adds:
- Mr. Market: "The manic-depressive Mr. Market does not always price stocks the way an appraiser or a private buyer would value a business." (C008) [full: C008#framework-1].
- "Mr. Market's job is to provide you with prices; your job is to decide whether it is to your advantage to act on them. You do not have to trade with him just because he constantly begs you to." (C008) [full: C008#rule-1]
- The individual's edge: "The investor who permits himself to be stampeded or unduly worried by unjustified market declines in his holdings is perversely transforming his basic advantage into a basic disadvantage." (C008) [full: C008#takeaway-2].
- A written "investment owner's contract" commits you never to act just because the market moved [full: C008#method-3].

Tests in this chapter: tangible-asset value plus at most one-third (Graham, 1972) [full: S008#rule-4]; a holding period of at least one year, ideally five, to cut taxes (Zweig, 2003) [full: C008#rule-5].

Use this chapter when: a price move, up or down, is driving the decision.

## Chapter 9: Investment funds

**In one line:** funds suit most people, but costs, size and fashion decide the result.

Graham says:
- "the average individual who put his money exclusively in investment-fund shares in the past ten years has fared better than the average person who made his common-stock purchases directly" (S009) [full: S009#takeaway-2]
- Prefer no-load funds [full: S009#rule-1]; ask for at least five years of record [full: S009#rule-2].
- "All financial experience up to now indicates that large funds, soundly managed, can produce at best only slightly better than average results over the years." (S009) [full: S009#takeaway-6]
- Closed-end funds: "If you want to put money in investment funds, buy a group of closed-end shares at a discount of, say, 10% to 15% from asset value" (S009) [full: S009#rule-4].
- The 1960s performance funds rose, then "In 1969 they all showed losses" (S009) [full: S009#takeaway-5].

Zweig's commentary adds:
- "the higher a fund's expenses, the lower its returns" (C009) [full: C009#rule-1]
- A typical fund must beat the market by 3.5 points a year before costs just to match it [full: C009#rule-5].
- Index funds with about 0.2% expenses have the edge [full: C009#rule-6].
- Choose in reverse order, costs first: "a fund's expenses are far more predictable than its future risk or return, you should make them your first filter." (C009) [full: C009#method-2].
- "If you're not prepared to stick with a fund through at least three lean years, you shouldn't buy it in the first place." (C009) [full: C009#rule-15]

Tests in this chapter: closed-end funds at a 10% to 15% discount (Graham, 1972) [full: S009#rule-4]; 3.5-point hurdle [full: C009#rule-5], 0.2% index expenses [full: C009#rule-6], managers owning at least 1% [full: C009#rule-7], three lean years of patience [full: C009#rule-15] (Zweig, 2003).

Use this chapter when: choosing or leaving a fund.

## Chapter 10: The investor and his advisers

**In one line:** advice helps avoid errors; it does not produce profits, and the client must understand it.

Graham says:
- "If the reason people invest is to make money, then in seeking advice they are asking others to tell them how to make money." (S010) [full: S010#takeaway-1]
- Brokers tend to help customers "help its customers make money in a field where they are condemned almost by mathematical law to lose in the end." (S010) [full: S010#warning-7].
- "Only in the exceptional case, where the integrity and competence of the advisers have been thoroughly demonstrated, should the investor act upon the advice of others without understanding and approving the decision made." (S010) [full: S010#rule-7]
- "They should be wary of all persons, whether customers’ brokers or security salesmen, who promise spectacular income or profits." (S010) [full: S010#warning-15]
- In a typical counsel account, no more than 10% goes outside leading companies [full: S010#rule-1].

Zweig's commentary adds:
- "TRUST, THEN VERIFY" (C010) [full: C010#framework-1]: check an adviser's record and paperwork first [full: C010#method-1].
- An adviser using technical analysis or market timing is a "no" signal [full: C010#rule-5].
- Fees above 1% of assets a year are too much [full: C010#rule-7]; promised returns above 8% to 10% are unrealistic [full: C010#rule-8].

Tests in this chapter: 10% outside leading companies (Graham, 1972) [full: S010#rule-1]; fee cap 1% and return claims capped at 8% to 10% (Zweig, 2003) [full: C010#rule-7].

Use this chapter when: someone is hiring, firing or blindly following an adviser or tip service.

## Chapter 11: Security analysis for the lay investor

**In one line:** test bonds by earnings coverage, value stocks with a margin of safety, and distrust long forecasts.

Graham says:
- Bond safety rests on coverage of interest by earnings over a period of years [full: S011#rule-1], with a check on the poorest year [full: S011#rule-5].
- Valuation starts "The now-standard procedure for estimating future earning power starts with average past data for physical volume, prices received, and operating margin." (S011) [full: S011#framework-5].
- "The higher the growth rate you project, and the longer the future period over which you project it, the more sensitive your forecast becomes to the slightest error." (S011) [full: S011#warning-4]
- The valuer builds in a margin of safety, acting "as an engineer does in his specifications for a structure." (S011) [full: S011#framework-7]
- The management factor is most "useful, we think, in those cases in which a recent change has taken place that has not yet had the time to show its significance in the actual figures." (S011) [full: S011#rule-10]
- "Stock of a company with a lot of surplus cash and nothing ahead of the common is clearly a better purchase (at the same price) than another one with the same per share earnings but large bank loans and senior securities." (S011) [full: S011#rule-11]
- "Whenever calculus is brought in, or higher algebra, you could take it as a warning signal that the operator was trying to substitute theory for experience" (S011) [full: S011#warning-5]

Zweig's commentary adds:
- Watch for companies that are OPM addicts [full: C011#warning-1]; negative operating cash with positive financing cash is a warning [full: C011#warning-3].
- "An average of more than two or three acquisitions a year is a sign of potential trouble." (C011) [full: C011#rule-1]
- "in general, long-term debt should be under 50% of total capital." (C011) [full: C011#rule-4]
- Owner earnings per share growing at least 6% or 7% a year over 10 years marks a steady cash generator [full: C011#rule-3].
- "A manager can't legitimately be your partner if he keeps selling while you're buying." (C011) [full: C011#warning-9]

Tests in this chapter (Graham, 1972, pretax coverage of interest, average and poorest year): utilities 4 and 3 times, railroads 5 and 4, industrials 7 and 5, retailers 5 and 4 [full: S011#rule-2]; poorest year about two-thirds of the seven-year test [full: S011#rule-5]; 20 years of dividends a plus [full: S011#rule-12]; growth estimated over 7 to 10 years [full: S011#rule-14]. Zweig (2003): long-term debt under 50% of capital [full: C011#rule-4]; more than two or three acquisitions a year is a warning [full: C011#rule-1].

Use this chapter when: checking whether a company can carry its debt, or how much a growth forecast can be trusted.

## Chapter 12: Per-share earnings

**In one line:** one year's earnings figure is a trap; use averages, full dilution and the footnotes.

Graham says:
- "Don't take a single year's earnings seriously." (S012) [full: S012#warning-1]
- "There is much more footnote material; in fact it occupies twice as much space as do the basic figures themselves." (S012) [full: S012#warning-3]
- Assume conversions where they would pay [full: S012#rule-1].
- Special charges dumped into a bad year: "What a nice arrangement, then, to charge as much as possible to the bad year, which had already been written off mentally" (S012) [full: S012#warning-7].
- Growth rate: "We suggest that the growth rate itself be calculated by comparing the average of the last three years with corresponding figures ten years earlier." (S012) [full: S012#method-1].
- "High multipliers have been maintained in the stock market only if the company has maintained better than average profitability." (S012) [full: S012#rule-4]

Zweig's commentary adds:
- "As an intelligent investor, the only thing you should do with pro forma earnings is ignore them." (C012) [full: C012#rule-1]
- Watch for nonrecurring costs that keep on recurring [full: C012#warning-5].
- "Read backwards. When you research a company’s financial reports, start reading on the last page and slowly work your way toward the front." (C012) [full: C012#method-1]
- "Never buy a stock without reading the footnotes to the financial statements in the annual report." (C012) [full: C012#rule-5]
- A pension return assumption above 6.5% was implausible as of 2003 [full: C012#rule-4].

Tests in this chapter: growth from three-year averages ten years apart (Graham) [full: S012#method-1]; pension assumption at most 6.5% (Zweig, 2003) [full: C012#rule-4].

Use this chapter when: an earnings number is the main argument for a decision.

## Chapter 14: Stock selection for the defensive investor

**In one line:** the seven tests, and why they exclude most stocks.

Graham says:
- Size: "not less than $100 million of annual sales for an industrial company and, not less than $50 million of total assets for a public utility." (S014) [full: S014#rule-1]
- Financial strength: current assets at least twice current liabilities (see Zweig's restatement below) and "We do require an adequate proportion of stock capital to debt." (S014) [full: S014#rule-10].
- "Some earnings for the common stock in each of the past ten years." (S014) [full: S014#rule-2]
- "Uninterrupted payments for at least the past 20 years." (S014) [full: S014#rule-3]
- "A minimum increase of at least one-third in per-share earnings in the past ten years using three-year averages at the beginning and end." (S014) [full: S014#rule-18]
- "Current price should not be more than 15 times average earnings of the past three years." (S014) [full: S014#rule-4]
- "Current price should not be more than 11⁄2 times the book value last reported." (S014) [full: S014#method-5]; and "the product of the multiplier times the ratio of price to book value should not exceed 22.5" (S014) [full: S014#rule-5].
- "These requirements are set up especially for the needs and the temperament of defensive investors. They will eliminate the great majority of common stocks as candidates for the portfolio" (S014) [full: S014#takeaway-1]
- On technology and growth, "Our advice to the defensive investor is that he let it alone. Let him emphasize diversification more than individual selection." (S014) [full: S014#rule-14].

Zweig's commentary adds:
- "Keep 90% of your stock money in an index fund, leaving 10% with which to try picking your own stocks." (C014) [full: C014#rule-1]
- Defensive investors should avoid companies worth less than $2 billion [full: C014#rule-2].
- Graham's growth hurdle is low: "33% cumulative growth over a decade is less than a 3% average annual increase" (C014) [full: C014#warning-4]. Zweig raises it to 50% over ten years [full: C014#rule-8].
- "it's nonsensical to derive a price/earnings ratio by dividing the known current price by unknown future earnings." (C014) [full: C014#warning-5]
- "The best values today are often found in the stocks that were once hot and have since gone cold. Throughout history, such stocks have often provided the margin of safety that a defensive investor demands." (C014) [full: C014#takeaway-5]

Tests in this chapter (Graham, 1972): sales at least $100 million (industrial) or assets $50 million (utility) [full: S014#rule-1]; earnings every year for ten years [full: S014#rule-2]; 20 years of dividends [full: S014#rule-3]; earnings growth of at least one-third in ten years [full: S014#rule-18]; P/E at most 15 on three-year average [full: S014#rule-4]; price at most 1.5 times book [full: S014#method-5]; P/E times price-to-book at most 22.5 [full: S014#rule-5]. Zweig (2003): long-term debt at most working capital [full: C014#rule-5]; institutional ownership above 60% suggests "overowned" [full: C014#rule-12]; 90% index, 10% stock picking [full: C014#rule-1].

Use this chapter when: building or checking a cautious stock list with hard filters.

## Chapter 15: Stock selection for the enterprising investor

**In one line:** beating the market is hard; Graham's own working method was net-current-asset bargains, plus a looser screen.

Graham says:
- "there is considerable and impressive evidence to the effect that this is very hard to do, even though the qualifications of those trying it are of the highest." (S015) [full: S015#takeaway-1]
- The bridge analogy: "Only a limited few can accomplish either aim." (S015) [full: S015#framework-2] When skill is equal, breaks decide the winners [full: S015#framework-2].
- His firm's method: purchases were "made typically at two-thirds or less of such stripped-down asset value." (S015) [full: S015#rule-1] He spread them across at least 100 issues [full: S015#rule-2].
- The screen: P/E of nine or less [full: S015#rule-4]; debt at most 110% of net current assets [full: S015#rule-5]; no deficit in five years [full: S015#rule-6]; some current dividend [full: S015#rule-7]; earnings above 1966 [full: S015#rule-8]; price under 120% of net tangible assets [full: S015#rule-9].
- Apply the tests less rigidly, "permitting a considerable plus in one factor to offset a small black mark in another." (S015) [full: S015#rule-3].
- "merger arbitrage is wholly inappropriate for most individual investors." (S015) [full: S015#warning-13]

Zweig's commentary adds:
- For most investors, picking single stocks is unnecessary [full: C015#rule-5].
- Keep any stock-picking basket to at most 10% of the portfolio, the rest in an index fund [full: C015#rule-1].
- An ROIC of at least 10% is attractive [full: C015#rule-2]; options limited to about 3% of shares is a good sign [full: C015#rule-3].
- Practise first: "He suggested starting off by spending a year tracking and picking stocks (but not with real money)." (C015) [full: C015#method-3].
- "First, they are disciplined and consistent, refusing to change their approach even when it is unfashionable." (C015) [full: C015#rule-9]

Tests in this chapter (Graham, 1972): two-thirds of net current asset value or less, 100+ issues [full: S015#rule-1]; the six-part screen above. Zweig (2003): 10% cap on stock picking [full: C015#rule-1]; ROIC 10% [full: C015#rule-2]; options 3% [full: C015#rule-3].

Use this chapter when: an active investor wants a concrete screen, or needs a reality check on beating the index.

## Chapter 16: Convertibles and warrants

**In one line:** conversion rights are no free lunch; test them in falling markets.

Graham says:
- "you cannot by a mere ingenious device make a bargain much better for both sides. In exchange for the conversion privilege the investor usually gives up something important in quality or yield, or both." (S016) [full: S016#warning-1]
- "the group of convertible issues floated during the latter part of a bull market are bound to yield unsatisfactory results as a whole." (S016) [full: S016#warning-2]
- Convertibles can only be judged by how they do in a falling market, and that has disappointed [full: S016#rule-2].
- The ideal: "a strongly secured convertible, exchangeable for a common stock which itself is attractive, and at a price only slightly higher than the current market." (S016) [full: S016#framework-1].
- Older issues beat new ones [full: S016#rule-3].
- Warrants: "We consider the recent development of stock-option warrants as a near fraud, an existing menace, and a potential disaster." (S016) [full: S016#warning-11]

Zweig's commentary adds:
- Zweig calls convertible bonds stocks for chickens [full: C016#takeaway-1].
- Converts can add stock-like return to a bond-heavy portfolio [full: C016#method-1], through a fund costing no more than 1.0% a year [full: C016#rule-1].
- Covered calls: "For individual investors, covering your downside is never worth surrendering most of your upside." (C016) [full: C016#rule-2].

Tests in this chapter: soundness tested in a declining market (Graham) [full: S016#rule-2]; convertible fund expenses at most 1.0% (Zweig, 2003) [full: C016#rule-1].

Use this chapter when: a hybrid security is offered as safety plus upside.

## Chapter 18: Eight pairs of companies

**In one line:** side-by-side pairs show that price, not just quality, decides investment merit.

Graham says:
- Air Products against Air Reduction: "Air Products sold at 161⁄2 times its latest earnings against only 9.1 times for Air Reduction. Also Air Products sold well above its asset backing, while Air Reduction could be bought at only 75% of its book value." (S018) [full: S018#rule-2].
- Flavors sold at 55 times earnings [full: S018#rule-3]; Harvester at a 41% discount to net worth [full: S018#rule-4], yet "Harvester's showing was too mediocre to make it really attractive even at its discount price." (S018) [full: S018#warning-11].
- "The low price of Harvester at the end of 1969 protected it from a large further decline in the bad break of 1970." (S018) [full: S018#takeaway-6]
- Good companies at high prices are still hard to short [full: S018#warning-9].

Zweig's commentary adds:
- "Although there are good and bad companies, there is no such thing as a good stock; there are only good stock prices, which come and go." (C018) [full: C018#takeaway-1]
- Serial acquirers, "which grow largely by buying other companies, nearly always meet a bad end on Wall Street." (C018) [full: C018#warning-2]

## Chapter 20: Margin of safety

**In one line:** the central idea: pay a price low enough that being wrong does not ruin you.

Graham says:
- "Confronted with a like challenge to distill the secret of sound investment into three words, we venture the motto, MARGIN OF SAFETY." (S020) [full: S020#framework-1]
- "Here the function of the margin of safety is, in essence, that of rendering unnecessary an accurate estimate of the future." (S020) [full: S020#takeaway-1]
- "The margin of safety is always dependent on the price paid. It will be large at one price, small at some higher price, nonexistent at some still higher price" (S020) [full: S020#takeaway-3]
- For stocks, the margin is earning power above the bond rate. Over ten years the typical excess "may aggregate 50% of the price paid. This figure is sufficient to provide a very real margin of safety" (S020) [full: S020#rule-4]
- With such a margin "in each of a diversified list of twenty or more stocks" (S020) [full: S020#rule-3], a good result becomes very likely.
- "the chief losses to investors come from the purchase of low-quality securities at times of favorable business conditions" (S020) [full: S020#warning-3]
- "operations for profit should be based not on optimism but on arithmetic." (S020) [full: S020#method-4]
- Know your business, and supervise anyone who runs it [full: S020#rule-10] [full: S020#rule-11].

Zweig's commentary adds:
- "you must take responsibility for ensuring that you never lose most or all of your money." (C020) [full: C020#rule-1]
- "The probability of making at least one mistake at some point in your investing lifetime is virtually 100%, and those odds are entirely out of your control." (C020) [full: C020#rule-2]
- "Ultimately, financial risk resides not in what kinds of investments you have, but in what kind of investor you are." (C020) [full: C020#takeaway-2]
- Pascal's wager: "In making decisions under conditions of uncertainty, the consequences must dominate the probabilities." (C020) [full: C020#framework-1].

Tests in this chapter (Graham, 1972): bond earnings coverage about five times [full: S020#rule-1]; twenty or more stocks [full: S020#rule-3]; excess earning power about 50% of price over ten years [full: S020#rule-4]; secondary stocks at two-thirds of indicated value [full: S020#rule-9].

Use this chapter when: any decision, as the last check before acting.

## The whole book in one framework

The path a reader follows, start to finish. Every step names the chapters that teach it.

1. **Define the act.** Is this an investment (thorough analysis, safety of principal, adequate return) or speculation? If speculation, cap it and keep it apart. Chapters 1 and 20; Zweig's 10% cap [full: C001#rule-7].
2. **Know yourself and pick a role.** Defensive or enterprising, chosen by effort and temperament, not by appetite for return. Chapters 1, 4 and 7 [full: S004#framework-1].
3. **Set the split and keep it.** Stocks between 25% and 75%, 50-50 as the default, rebalanced on drift or by date, changed only when life changes. Chapters 1 to 4 [full: S001#rule-1] [full: C004#rule-4].
4. **Price the market, do not time it.** Compare earnings yield with bond yield, expect modest returns from a high level, use swings only to act on value. Chapters 3 and 8 [full: S008#framework-1].
5. **Pick the vehicle.** Defensive investors use funds, index funds or the seven tests; check costs before records. Chapters 5, 9 and 14 [full: C009#method-2].
6. **Screen the business.** Size, strength, earnings record, dividends, growth, moderate P/E and price to book; for the active investor, the looser Chapter 15 screen or net-current-asset bargains. Chapters 7, 13, 14 and 15 [full: S014#rule-5].
7. **Check the numbers behind the numbers.** Averages not single years, full dilution, footnotes, debt coverage, cash flow. Chapters 11, 12 and 17 [full: S012#warning-1].
8. **Avoid the known traps.** New issues, second-grade bonds at full price, convertibles and warrants late in a boom, serial acquirers, hired advice you cannot judge. Chapters 6, 10, 16 and 17 [full: S006#rule-8].
9. **Act as an owner afterwards.** Read the proxy, ask for a payout or proof, watch pay and dilution. Chapter 19 [full: C019#framework-2].
10. **Demand a margin of safety and diversify.** The price must leave room for error, spread over many holdings, so that a mistake does not ruin you. Chapter 20 [full: S020#takeaway-3] [full: C020#rule-2].
