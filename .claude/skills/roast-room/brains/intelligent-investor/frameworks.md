## 1. Margin of safety

- **What it is (Graham):** Graham's three-word summary of sound investment. A price far enough below value, or an earning power far enough above the bond rate, that errors and bad luck can be absorbed. Graham: "we venture the motto, MARGIN OF SAFETY" (S020). Its function is "rendering unnecessary an accurate estimate of the future" (S020) [full: S020#takeaway-1].
- **Steps (Graham):**
  1. For a bond, compare the earnings available with the fixed charges over a period of years, or compare the total value of the enterprise with the debt [full: S020#method-2].
  2. For a common stock, compare the earnings rate on the price paid with the bond interest rate [full: S020#framework-2].
  3. Remember the margin depends on price: "It will be large at one price, small at some higher price, nonexistent at some still higher price" (S020) [full: S020#takeaway-3].
  4. Demand that the margin be shown in figures and experience, not hope [full: S020#takeaway-5].
  5. Diversify, because the margin works on a group, not on each single stock [full: S020#framework-5].
- **When to use:** Every decision. Graham calls it the touchstone between investment and speculation [full: S020#framework-6].
- **How to score (guidance, limits as printed):**
  - Graham, 1972 US: over ten years the excess of stock earning power over bond interest "may aggregate 50% of the price paid" (S020), which he calls sufficient [full: S020#rule-4].
  - Graham, 1972 US: with a margin present in each of "twenty or more stocks" (S020) the chance of a good result under fairly normal conditions becomes very large [full: S020#rule-3].
  - Graham, 1972 US: for secondary stocks, buy at "two-thirds or less of their indicated value" (S020) [full: S020#rule-9].
  - Graham: estimates that differ from the past "must err at least slightly on the side of understatement" (S020) [full: S020#rule-6].
  - Zweig: the probability of at least one mistake in a lifetime is "virtually 100%" (C020), so the margin protects against the consequences of being wrong [full: C020#rule-2].
- **Quote:** "a true margin of safety is one that can be demonstrated by figures, by persuasive reasoning, and by reference to a body of actual experience" (S020) [full: S020#takeaway-5]
- **Rests on:** S020#framework-1, S020#framework-2, S020#framework-4, S020#framework-5, S020#framework-6, S020#takeaway-1, S020#takeaway-3, S020#takeaway-5, S020#rule-3, S020#rule-4, S020#rule-9, S011#framework-7, S014#rule-23, C020#takeaway-1, C020#rule-2, C014#takeaway-5. ## 2. Investment versus speculation

- **What it is (Graham):** The book's opening definition. "An investment operation is one which, upon thorough analysis promises safety of principal and an adequate return" (S001). Anything else is speculative [full: S001#framework-1].
- **Parts (Zweig's reading, C001):** Zweig splits the definition into three requirements: thorough analysis of the business before buying [full: C001#rule-1], deliberate protection against serious losses [full: C001#rule-2], and an aim of adequate, not extraordinary, performance [full: C001#rule-3].
- **Steps:**
  1. Graham: separate any speculation completely. "Never mingle your speculative and investment operations in the same account, nor in any part of your thinking" (S001) [full: S001#warning-5].
  2. Graham: name the three forms of unintelligent speculation: speculating while thinking you invest, speculating seriously without skill, and risking more than you can afford [full: S001#warning-3].
  3. Graham: judge by attitude to price. The speculator wants to profit from fluctuations; the investor wants "acquiring and holding suitable securities at suitable prices" (S008) [full: S008#takeaway-7].
  4. Zweig: apply the comfort test: own a stock only if you would be comfortable not knowing its daily price [full: C001#rule-4].
- **When to use:** Before any purchase, and to sort a portfolio into an investment account and a separate speculation account.
- **How to score (guidance):**
  - Zweig, 2003: "10% of our overall wealth is the maximum permissible amount to put at speculative risk" (C001) [full: C001#rule-7].
  - Graham, 1972 US: in the typical case no more than 10% of a fund goes outside leading companies [full: S010#rule-1].
  - Graham: anyone who buys a hot new issue "is either speculating or gambling" (S001) [full: S001#warning-4].
- **Quote:** "You must never delude yourself into thinking that you're investing when you're speculating." (C001) [full: C001#rule-11]
- **Rests on:** S001#framework-1, S001#framework-2, S001#warning-3, S001#warning-4, S001#warning-5, S008#takeaway-7, S008#rule-1, S020#framework-6, C001#framework-1, C001#framework-2, C001#rule-1, C001#rule-2, C001#rule-3, C001#rule-4, C001#rule-7, C001#rule-11, S010#rule-1. ## 3. Defensive versus enterprising investor

- **What it is (Graham):** The book's master split. The investor first chooses a role, defensive (passive) or aggressive (enterprising), and every later rule depends on that choice [full: S007#rule-25]. The split is by effort and skill, not by appetite for risk.
- **Parts:**
  - Graham: the defensive investor wants safety and freedom from bother; the enterprising investor is willing to give time and care for a better return [full: S004#rule-11].
  - Graham: most people should be defensive. "The majority of security owners should elect the defensive classification" (S007) [full: S007#rule-9].
  - Graham: the enterprising investor needs "a considerable knowledge of security values" (S007), enough to treat investing as a business [full: S007#rule-8].
  - Zweig: the active approach "takes lots of time and loads of energy" (C004), while the passive one needs little effort but great detachment [full: C004#framework-1].
- **Steps:**
  1. Graham: decide honestly which role you can carry. A beginner tests judgment first "with the smallest possible sums" (S005) [full: S005#method-3].
  2. Graham: a defensive investor stays inside the safe and narrow path of standard investment [full: S020#framework-8].
  3. Graham: an investor moves to the enterprising class only when competent to judge for himself [full: S010#takeaway-6].
- **When to use:** At the start, before choosing any screen. Chapter 14's tests belong to the defensive investor; Chapter 15's belong to the enterprising one.
- **How to score (guidance):**
  - Graham, 1972 US: the extra search is worth the effort only if it could add "say, 5% before taxes to the average annual return" (S001) [full: S001#rule-9].
  - Graham: the defensive investor should avoid foreign bonds, ordinary preferreds and secondary common stocks regardless of price; the enterprising investor buys them only at bargain prices [full: S007#rule-11] [full: S007#rule-12].
- **Quote:** "To achieve satisfactory investment results is easier than most people realize; to achieve superior results is harder than it looks." (S020) [full: S020#takeaway-7]
- **Rests on:** S004#rule-11, S005#method-3, S007#rule-8, S007#rule-9, S007#rule-11, S007#rule-12, S007#rule-25, S010#takeaway-6, S020#framework-8, S020#takeaway-7, S001#rule-9, C004#framework-1. Sections: S001, S004, S005, S007, S010, S020, C004 (7).

## 4. The stock and bond split

- **What it is (Graham):** The defensive investor's first policy decision: how to divide the fund between high-grade bonds and leading stocks [full: S001#framework-3].
- **Steps (Graham):**
  1. Keep each part inside a band: "the proportion held in bonds be never less than 25% or more than 75%" (S001) [full: S001#rule-1].
  2. The simplest choice is a 50-50 split, restored when market moves disturb it by about 5% [full: S001#method-1].
  3. Move toward 25% stocks if the market looks dangerously high and toward 75% if falling prices make stocks more attractive [full: S001#rule-2] [full: S001#rule-3].
  4. Graham's 1972 view: no more than 50% in stocks "at this time" (S004) [full: S004#rule-2].
- **Zweig's restatement:** Zweig keeps the same band and says everyone should hold at least 25% in bonds, because few people can hold stocks in a falling market [full: C004#rule-2]. He turns the formula into rebalancing on a fixed schedule [full: C004#method-2].
- **When to use:** At the start, and at each scheduled review.
- **How to score (guidance, 1972 US):** stocks below 25% or above 75% of the fund is outside Graham's band; a move of about 5% from 50-50 is his rebalancing trigger [full: S001#rule-15]. Graham's expected 1972 return on the mix was about 7.8% before taxes [full: S001#takeaway-10].
- **Quote:** "the investor should never have less than 25% or more than 75% of his funds in common stocks" (S004) [full: S004#rule-1]
- **Rests on:** S001#framework-3, S001#framework-4, S001#method-1, S001#rule-1, S001#rule-2, S001#rule-3, S001#rule-15, S004#rule-1, S004#rule-2, S005#framework-1, S007#method-3, S003#rule-4, C004#rule-2, C004#rule-3, C004#method-2. Sections: S001, S003, S004, S005, S007, C004 (6).

## 5. Market fluctuations: the owner's double status and Mr. Market

- **What it is:**
  - Graham: the holder of listed shares has a "double status" (S008), as part owner of a business and as holder of a quoted piece of paper he may use or ignore [full: S008#framework-4]. "Basically, price fluctuations have only one significant meaning for the true investor." (S008) [full: S008#rule-8]
  - Zweig names this figure Mr. Market and describes him as "manic-depressive" (C008), a partner who does not price shares the way a private buyer would value the business [full: C008#framework-1].
- **Steps:**
  1. Graham: value holdings from the company's reports on its operations and finances, not from the quotation [full: S008#rule-17].
  2. Graham: a price fall is no loss unless the underlying value has shrunk [full: S008#rule-7].
  3. Graham: use big moves to buy wisely when prices fall sharply and sell wisely when they rise a great deal; otherwise pay attention to dividends and operating results [full: S008#rule-8] [full: S008#rule-9].
  4. Graham: never buy a stock because it has gone up or sell one because it has gone down [full: S008#rule-11].
  5. Zweig: "Mr. Market's job is to provide you with prices; your job is to decide whether it is to your advantage to act on them." (C008) [full: C008#rule-1]
- **When to use:** Whenever a price move, not a change in the business, is prompting an action.
- **How to score (guidance):**
  - Graham, 1972 US: expect most holdings to rise "50% or more from their low point" (S008) and fall one-third or more from their high [full: S008#warning-8].
  - Graham: "a decline of 50% fully offsets a preceding advance of 100%" (S008) [full: S008#rule-16].
  - Zweig: hold stocks at least one year [full: C008#rule-5].
- **Quote:** "By refusing to let Mr. Market be your master, you transform him into your servant." (C008) [full: C008#framework-2]
- **Rests on:** S008#framework-4, S008#framework-7, S008#rule-7, S008#rule-8, S008#rule-9, S008#rule-11, S008#rule-17, S008#warning-8, S008#warning-11, C008#framework-1, C008#framework-2, C008#rule-1, C008#rule-12, C020#takeaway-5. Sections: S008, C008, C020 (3). Note: the extracted items carry Zweig's Mr. Market wording; Graham's own Mr. Market parable text is not among them (see the list at the end).

## 6. Pricing versus timing

- **What it is (Graham):** Two ways to act on market moves. Timing means forecasting; pricing means buying below value and selling above it [full: S008#framework-1].
- **Steps (Graham):**
  1. Reject forecasting: the average investor "cannot deal successfully with price movements by endeavoring to forecast them" (S008) [full: S008#takeaway-2].
  2. Timing has value only when it lets you buy back "at substantially under his previous selling price" (S008) [full: S008#rule-3].
  3. The defensive minimum is simply not to pay too much [full: S008#rule-2].
  4. Buy whenever money is available, except when the market is much higher than established standards of value justify [full: S008#rule-10].
- **When to use:** When a market call is being offered as a reason to act.
- **How to score (guidance):** a decision that depends on a forecast of the market's direction fails Graham's test; one that depends on price against value passes it.
- **Quote:** "if he places his emphasis on timing, in the sense of forecasting, he will end up as a speculator" (S008) [full: S008#warning-2]
- **Rests on:** S008#framework-1, S008#takeaway-1, S008#takeaway-2, S008#warning-2, S008#warning-3, S008#rule-2, S008#rule-3, S008#rule-10, S011#takeaway-5. Sections: S008, S011 (2).

## 7. The seven statistical tests for the defensive investor

- **What it is (Graham, Chapter 14):** A set of seven tests applied to each stock bought by a defensive investor [full: S014#framework-1]. Graham says they "will eliminate the great majority of common stocks" (S014) [full: S014#takeaway-1]. The full line-by-line list with limits is in `checks.md` section A.
- **Steps (Graham, 1972 US):** adequate size; strong financial condition; earnings in each of ten years; twenty years of dividends; earnings growth of at least one-third over ten years; price no more than 15 times three-year average earnings; price no more than 1.5 times book value, or a product of multiplier and price-to-book no higher than 22.5 [full: S014#rule-1] [full: S014#rule-2] [full: S014#rule-3] [full: S014#rule-18] [full: S014#rule-4] [full: S014#rule-24] [full: S014#rule-5].
- **Zweig's update (2003):** Zweig keeps the tests and restates some: market value of at least $2 billion [full: C014#rule-2]; current ratio of 2 to 1 and long-term debt no higher than working capital [full: C014#rule-4] [full: C014#rule-5]; and the 22.5 product [full: C014#rule-11].
- **When to use:** Building or reviewing a defensive portfolio of individual stocks.
- **How to score (guidance):** count the tests passed. Graham's own worked case: only five of the thirty Dow stocks passed all seven in 1970 to 1971 [full: S014#takeaway-2]. Graham allowed a small deficiency to be waived where another figure compensated (Swift) [full: S014#method-6].
- **Quote:** "the product of the multiplier times the ratio of price to book value should not exceed 22.5" (S014) [full: S014#rule-5]

## 8. The four rules for the defensive investor's common stocks

- **What it is (Graham, Chapter 5):** Four general rules that come before the Chapter 14 tests: diversify adequately, buy large, prominent and conservatively financed companies, require a long dividend record, and set a price limit on earnings [full: S005#rule-1] [full: S005#rule-2] [full: S005#rule-12] [full: S005#rule-10]. Graham refers back to these four in Chapter 14 [full: S014#rule-15].
- **Steps (Graham, 1972 US):**
  1. Hold "a minimum of ten different issues and a maximum of about thirty" (S005) [full: S005#rule-1].
  2. Large means about $50 million of assets or business [full: S005#rule-8]; prominent means in the first quarter or first third of its industry by size [full: S005#rule-9].
  3. Conservative finance: for an industrial, common stock at book value is at least half of total capital including bank debt; for a railroad or utility at least 30% [full: S005#rule-6] [full: S005#rule-7].
  4. Price no more than 25 times seven-year average earnings and no more than 20 times the last twelve months [full: S005#rule-10].
- **Zweig's updates:** Zweig keeps ten to thirty stocks as a starting point and adds a warning against being overexposed to one industry [full: C005#rule-1]. The store also marks a $10 billion market-value bar on page 137 as Graham, but its wording ("In today's markets" (S005)) reads as Zweig's 2003 footnote; unchecked against the page [full: S005#rule-20].
- **When to use:** Before Chapter 14's tests; also as the minimum for anyone picking stocks.
- **How to score (guidance):** each rule is pass or fail on its printed limit; Graham warns against applying such criteria rigidly [full: S005#warning-7].
- **Quote:** "Each company selected should be large, prominent, and conservatively financed." (S005) [full: S005#rule-2]

## 9. Net-current-asset bargains

- **What it is (Graham):** The most easily identified bargain: a stock selling below the company's net working capital alone, after deducting all prior obligations [full: S007#method-2]. Zweig explains the term: current assets less total liabilities, per share; Graham's followers call these "net nets" [full: S015#framework-4] [full: C007#method-2].
- **Steps:**
  1. Graham: compute current assets minus all liabilities, per share; compare with the price [full: S007#rule-7].
  2. Graham: buy, as Graham-Newman did, at "two-thirds or less of such stripped-down asset value" (S015) [full: S015#rule-1].
  3. Graham: diversify widely: "at least 100 different issues" (S015) in Graham-Newman's practice [full: S015#rule-2].
  4. Graham's 1970 screen excluded companies with a net loss in the latest twelve months [full: S015#warning-16].
  5. Zweig: buy them only "a couple dozen at a time" (C007) and hold patiently [full: C007#rule-4].
- **When to use:** Enterprising investor only; Graham notes such opportunities had dried up during most of the decade before 1972 [full: S015#takeaway-9].
- **How to score (guidance, 1972 US):** price at or below net current asset value per share is the test; two-thirds of it was Graham-Newman's buying level.
- **Quote:** "the record of common-stock purchases made at a price below their working-capital value has no such bad mark against it" (S015) [full: S015#takeaway-9]
- **Rests on:** S007#method-2, S007#rule-7, S015#framework-4, S015#rule-1, S015#rule-2, S015#rule-17, S015#method-8, S015#takeaway-9, S015#warning-16, S018#rule-5, S001#example-5, S008#example-5, C007#method-1, C007#method-2, C007#rule-4. Sections: S001, S007, S008, S015, S018, C007 (6).

## 10. The enterprising investor's winnowing screen

- **What it is (Graham, Chapter 15):** A mechanical screen of the Standard & Poor's Stock Guide, starting from a low price against recent earnings and adding tests of financial condition, stability, dividends, growth and asset value [full: S015#method-2]. The line-by-line list is in `checks.md` section B.
- **Steps (Graham, 1970-71 US):** price at nine times earnings or less [full: S015#rule-4]; debt no more than 110% of net current assets for industrials [full: S015#rule-5]; no deficit in five years [full: S015#rule-6]; some current dividend [full: S015#rule-7]; last year's earnings above 1966's [full: S015#rule-8]; price below 120% of net tangible assets [full: S015#rule-9]; optionally an S&P quality rank of average or better [full: S015#framework-3].
- **When to use:** By the enterprising investor, applied to a group, not to one stock; Graham set no lower size limit because small companies "may afford enough safety if bought carefully and on a group basis" (S015) [full: S015#rule-14].
- **How to score (guidance):** count tests passed; Graham allows "a considerable plus in one factor to offset a small black mark in another" (S015) [full: S015#rule-3]. His estimate: about 150 of 4,500 companies would pass all six [full: S015#takeaway-5].
- **Quote:** "a group of issues, of at least average quality, meeting criteria of financial condition as well, purchasable at a low multiplier of current earnings and below asset value, should offer good promise of satisfactory investment results" (S015) [full: S015#takeaway-14]

## 11. Bargain issues and appraisal

- **What it is (Graham):** A bargain is a security whose value, found by analysis, is well above its price. Graham names two ways to estimate value: appraisal (future earnings times a suitable factor) and the value of the business to a private owner [full: S007#method-5] [full: S007#method-6].
- **Steps (Graham):**
  1. Estimate value by appraisal or private-owner value.
  2. Call it a true bargain only if "the indicated value is at least 50% more than the price" (S007) [full: S007#rule-5].
  3. For cheap cyclicals, require the price to be low against past average earnings, not only current earnings [full: S007#rule-4].
  4. Add stability: no year of deficit over a decade or more, plus size and financial strength [full: S007#rule-6].
- **When to use:** Enterprising investor, especially at low points of the general market, when many stocks qualify [full: S007#rule-21].
- **How to score (guidance, 1972 US):** value at least 1.5 times price is Graham's bar; for secondary stocks the price should be no more than two-thirds of appraisal value [full: S007#rule-14].
- **Quote:** "At low points in the general market a large proportion of common stocks are bargain issues, as measured by these standards." (S007) [full: S007#rule-21]
- **Rests on:** S007#method-1, S007#method-5, S007#method-6, S007#rule-4, S007#rule-5, S007#rule-6, S007#rule-14, S007#rule-21, S007#framework-5, S020#takeaway-4. Sections: S007, S020 (2).

## 12. The relatively unpopular large company

- **What it is (Graham):** The enterprising investor concentrates on large companies passing through a period of unpopularity [full: S007#framework-2]. The ideal is a large, prominent company "selling both well below its past average price and its past average price/earnings multiplier" (S007) [full: S007#framework-3].
- **Steps:**
  1. Graham: start from the low-multiplier idea, then add other quantitative and qualitative tests [full: S007#method-1].
  2. Graham's test case: buying the six or ten Dow stocks at the lowest multipliers each year [full: S007#rule-2]. Zweig notes this is now called the "Dogs of the Dow" [full: S007#framework-9].
  3. Zweig: look at lists of stocks hitting new lows, because many professionals first get interested when a price goes down [full: C007#method-1] [full: C015#takeaway-4].
- **When to use:** Enterprising investor looking for undervalued leaders.
- **How to score (guidance):** price below its own past average and below its own past average multiplier; Zweig adds that the best values are often stocks "that were once hot and have since gone cold" (C014) [full: C014#takeaway-5].
- **Quote:** "The key requirement here is that the enterprising investor concentrate on the larger companies that are going through a period of unpopularity." (S007) [full: S007#framework-2]

## 13. Secondary companies and the insider-outsider gap

- **What it is (Graham):** A secondary company is not a leader in a fairly important industry: usually a smaller concern, or the chief unit of an unimportant line [full: S007#framework-4]. Its detached shares usually sell well below their worth to a controlling owner [full: S007#framework-8].
- **Steps (Graham):**
  1. Buy secondary issues only at bargain prices, on a diversified basis [full: S007#method-8] [full: S018#framework-1].
  2. Price them by the normal central market levels for that class, not by leader standards [full: S007#method-11].
  3. Expect profit from a high dividend return, substantial reinvested earnings, and recognition of value over time [full: S007#framework-5].
- **When to use:** Enterprising investor; the defensive investor avoids secondary stocks regardless of price [full: S007#rule-12].
- **How to score (guidance, 1972 US):** price "not more than two-thirds of the appraisal value" (S007) [full: S007#rule-14]; a boundary stock between leader and secondary may be bought "at a small discount" (S007) from appraisal value [full: S007#rule-15].
- **Quote:** "In secondary companies the average market value of a detached share is substantially less than its worth to a controlling owner." (S007) [full: S007#framework-8]
- **Rests on:** S007#framework-4, S007#framework-5, S007#framework-8, S007#method-8, S007#method-11, S007#rule-12, S007#rule-14, S007#rule-15, S018#framework-1, S018#example-11, S020#rule-9. Sections: S007, S018, S020 (3).

## 14. Earnings-power valuation: the two-part appraisal

- **What it is (Graham):** The analyst's standard procedure: estimate average future earnings, then multiply by a "capitalization factor" [full: S011#rule-20]. Graham proposes two parts: a value from past performance alone, then a judgment of how far new conditions should change it [full: S011#method-4] [full: S012#method-2].
- **Steps (Graham):**
  1. Start from average past data for volume, prices received and operating margin [full: S011#framework-5].
  2. Measure growth by comparing the last three years' average with the same figure ten years earlier [full: S012#method-1].
  3. Use the growth expected "over the next seven to ten years" (S011) [full: S011#rule-14].

## 15. Earnings yield against the bond rate

- **What it is (Graham):** A portfolio-level price test. Graham uses the same comparison to define the margin of safety on common stocks [full: S020#framework-2].
- **Steps:**

  2. Graham: compare it with the high-grade bond yield.
  3. Worked 2003 update in a Graham chapter, store author Graham but wording dated 2003, so it reads as Zweig's footnote (unchecked against the page): an AA bond yield of about 4.6% gives a suggested maximum P/E of 21.7 [full: S014#rule-27] [full: S014#rule-28].

- **How to score (guidance):** portfolio earnings yield at or above the high-grade bond rate passes. Graham, 1972: "Now [in 1972] there is no difference between the earnings rate on stocks and the interest rate on stocks" (S020), so he saw no margin [full: S020#takeaway-2].

## 16. Bond and preferred-stock safety tests

- **What it is (Graham, Chapter 11):** Bond safety rests mainly on how many times total interest charges were covered by earnings over a period of years, with a test of the poorest year [full: S011#rule-1] [full: S011#method-2]. The same figures apply to preferred stock, counting twice the preferred dividends [full: S011#rule-3].
- **Steps (Graham, 1972 US):**
  1. Compute pretax coverage of fixed charges, average over seven years and in the poorest year; the limits by type of business are in `rules.md` section D [full: S011#rule-2].
  2. Check size of enterprise [full: S011#rule-6] and the ratio of the market value of junior stock to debt (the "cushion") [full: S011#rule-7].
  3. For industrials, buy only companies of major size that have withstood a serious depression [full: S011#rule-9].
  4. Test coverage over a full cycle, preferably including a subnormal period such as 1970-71 [full: S020#rule-5].
- **Zweig:** his WorldCom case, where pretax income fell short of covering fixed charges in two of five years [full: C006#rule-1]; long-term debt should generally be under 50% of total capital [full: C011#rule-4].
- **When to use:** Any bond or preferred stock; also as a strength test on a company whose common stock is being considered.
- **How to score (guidance):** coverage at or above the printed multiple for the business type passes; Graham's railroad example earned charges "only 1.5 times in 1963, against our requirement of 5 times" (S006) [full: S006#rule-3].
- **Quote:** "Experience clearly shows that it is unwise to buy a bond or a preferred which lacks adequate safety merely because the yield is attractive." (S006) [full: S006#warning-2]
- **Rests on:** S011#rule-1 to S011#rule-9, S011#method-2, S011#framework-2, S011#framework-4, S020#rule-1, S020#rule-5, S020#method-2, S006#rule-3, S006#warning-2, C006#rule-1, C011#rule-4. Sections: S006, S011, S020, C006, C011 (5).

## 21. Pascal's wager: consequences over probabilities (Zweig)

- **What it is (Zweig, C020):** Since mistakes are certain, set up the portfolio so that being wrong is never ruinous.
- **Steps (Zweig):**
  1. Before investing, assess your chance of being right and how you will react if wrong [full: C020#rule-4].
  2. Ask whether other investments will tide you over if this one fails [full: C020#takeaway-4].

- **When to use:** Sizing any single commitment.
- **How to score (guidance):** Zweig's example of the wrong side of the wager: 85% of a portfolio in a single speculative sector [full: C020#warning-6].
