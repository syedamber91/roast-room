# Checks: The Intelligent Investor (Graham, with Zweig's commentary)

Built from 2,291 verified items in the private store (roast-room-private/intelligent-investor); every limit quoted below was checked word for word against its page.

The two checklists a reader of the book runs on a stock: (A) the defensive investor's, from Chapters 5 and 14, and (B) the enterprising investor's, from Chapters 7 and 15. Each line gives the test, the limit as printed (a word-for-word quote with its section id), the page, and the item id.

How to use them:
- Run one checklist, not both. Graham asks the investor to choose a role first [full: S007#rule-25]. The defensive list is for someone who wants safety and little effort; the enterprising list is for someone treating investing as a business.
- Every limit is a worked historical bar: Graham's are for the United States around 1970 to 1972, Zweig's for early 2003. They are not current Indian thresholds. Dollar sizes, bond yields and rating services do not convert to India on the book's own terms, and this seat does not convert them.
- A failed line is a reason to read further, not a verdict. Graham himself waived a small shortfall for Swift where a good 1970 showing offset it [full: S014#method-6], and for the enterprising investor he allows "a considerable plus in one factor to offset a small black mark in another" (S015) [full: S015#rule-3].
- Nothing here says to buy, sell or hold anything. It records which of the book's tests a company meets.

## C. What the book does not test

The items carry no numeric test for these, so neither checklist can score them:

- **Moats and competitive position.** No item gives a test for durable advantage; Graham's quality tests are size, prominence, stability, dividends and finance.
- **Management quality as a number.** Graham says the management factor matters most after a recent change not yet in the figures [full: S011#rule-10] and warns against counting it twice [full: S011#warning-14]; he states no measure. Zweig's lines (option dilution, acquisitions, proxy reading) are signs to read, not a score.
- **Future growth.** Graham doubts any realistic valuation for growth above about 8% a year [full: S011#warning-16]; neither checklist tests a forecast.
- **Industry outlook.** Graham minimises the practical value of most industry studies [full: S011#takeaway-6]; no checklist line rates an industry.
- **Market timing.** Both checklists are price tests on the company or portfolio, not forecasts of the market [full: S008#takeaway-2].
- **Banks, insurers and lenders as such.** Graham applies the same price-to-earnings and book tests to financial companies and stresses soundness [full: S014#rule-12] [full: S014#framework-2], but the items give no lender-specific ratio.
- **Cash flow.** Graham's tests use earnings, dividends and balance-sheet items. Cash flow enters only through Zweig, who asks whether operating cash flow grew steadily over ten years [full: C011#method-1]; he gives no limit.
- **Current-ratio half of the Chapter 15 screen.** Only the debt half (110% of net current assets) is in the items.
- **Anything specific to India.** The book is about the United States market; its figures (dollar sizes, US bond yields, S&P rankings, US tax brackets) have no Indian equivalent in the items.
