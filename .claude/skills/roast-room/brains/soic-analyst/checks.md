# Checks: SOIC Analyst

The analyst's checklist of what to notice before anyone acts on a listed Indian company: every forensic, accounting, ratio and balance-sheet check the course teaches, merged across sources, with the number of sources that teach each one. Entries are dated by their source (2026-07-14 to 2026-07-28). Each check detects something; none gates a verdict on its own and none is advice to buy, sell or hold.

## Contents

1. Earnings quality and cash conversion (entries 1 to 9)
2. Accounting red flags: revenue recognition, capitalisation, related parties, auditor, contingent liabilities (entries 10 to 17)
3. Balance sheet and leverage (entries 18 to 23)
4. Working capital and activity ratios (entries 24 to 27)
5. Return ratios and DuPont (entries 28 to 32)
6. Promoter, pledge, governance and shareholding (entries 33 to 37)
7. Cash flow and capex (entries 38 to 40)
8. Lenders and insurers: asset quality, reserving, NIM, solvency (entries 41 to 45)
9. Cyclicals and capital-cycle checks (entries 46 to 51)
10. Management and disclosure quality (entries 52 to 57)

## How to read an entry

- **RULE** means the course states the figure as a rule or working standard. **EXAMPLE** means a worked example or one company's snapshot. **ASSUMPTION** means the course says "let's assume". **PERISHABLE** marks a company figure or dated level that was true on the lecture date only and must be re-read from live data before use.
- Where two sources give different figures for the same thing, both are shown with ids and dates.
- "The course does not say" marks a gap. Nothing here fills a gap from outside the course.
- Source ids are the only provenance. A lesson code and time follow each quote.
- A check that fires is a prompt to read the filing, not a finding about the company.

## 1. Earnings quality and cash conversion

### 1. Cash conversion: CFO against EBITDA and PAT, over three to five years
- **Check:** Divide operating cash flow by EBITDA and by PAT, for several years, and watch the trend as well as the level.
- **Detects:** Profit that exists only as accruals. The course treats a falling trend as the early warning.
- **Red flag:** Conversion "begins to rapidly decline"; a level below the band; a fast-growing company whose conversion fell while receivables and inventory grew; operating profit to operating cash flow conversion falling, called a major red flag (S055); poor conversion with negative operating cash flow from an inventory build (S345, S348). In agrochemicals poor CFO to EBITDA is a standing sector trait (S341).
- **Green flag:** Stable conversion at or above the band over several years.
- **Where the data is:** Cash-flow statement and EBITDA in the annual report; the screener cash-flow and ratio grid. Cash-flow statements arrive with Q2 (H1) and Q4 (H2) results (S050).
- **Thresholds:**
  - RULE, dated 2026-07-28 (S001): CFO/EBITDA "ideally" about 70 percent for B2C and 60 percent for B2B. S001 frames this as a veto-class gate (F21). The same figures appear in S036, S045 and S050 (2026-07-27). S050 says 60 percent is acceptable for B2B because of higher inventory and looser credit.
  - COMPILED RULE (compiled list, not stated in a lecture note), dated 2026-07-28 (S002): 3-year average CFO/EBITDA at or above 60.
  - RULE, a second pair (S001 F38, S494 dated 2026-07-27): CFO/PAT above roughly 90 to 95 percent and CFO/EBITDA above roughly 60 to 65 percent. S001 says to treat both pairs as calibration ranges, not one authoritative number.
  - CFO/PAT is stated four ways: above 80 to 90 percent (S001, S470); above 70 percent for B2C and above 60 percent for B2B (S045); 60 to 70 percent of PAT adequate for B2B and 80 to 90 percent for B2C (S452); above 90 to 95 percent (S494).
  - S050: subtract other income from EBITDA before the ratio, because it "does not reflect core cash operating profit".
  - EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S009, 2026-07-14): OCF/EBITDA fell from 97 to 100 percent plus in FY23 to FY24 to about 65 percent in FY25, with contract assets rising from 661mn to 4,268mn and no expected-credit-loss provision.
  - EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S010, 2026-07-14): screener's CFO/OP gave 91.3 percent over three years while filings gave 63.5 percent. The course's reading is to use the stricter figure when inputs differ.
  - EXAMPLE (S045): an FMCG company converting only 50 percent of PAT falls short of the 70 percent benchmark.
- **Quote:** "should ideally be around 70% for consumer-facing B2C businesses and 60% for B2B businesses" (S001, MODULB 01:58:04)
- **Sources:** 14 (S001, S002, S009, S010, S036, S045, S050, S055, S341, S345, S348, S452, S470, S494)

### 2. Profit against cash: cumulative CFO beside cumulative PAT
- **Check:** Put five years of operating cash flow next to five years of net profit, then ask how the next capex is funded.
- **Detects:** Cash trapped in inventory or receivables, and the dilution that follows when internal cash cannot fund growth.
- **Red flag:** High profit with large negative cash flow over multiple years (S470); a falling CFO-to-PAT trend (S043) and falling conversion of operating profit into cash (S055); an early-stage company with weak CFO may improve, but "you have to track your debt" (S160); frequent equity dilution to fund operations that do not generate cash (S452); sales growing while operating cash flow turns negative on inventory build.
- **Green flag:** Cash operating profit above reported profit once depreciation, a non-cash charge, is added back (S453).
- **Where the data is:** Cash-flow statement, balance sheet, notes, auditor report (S470).
- **Thresholds:** The course states no numeric cut for this check beyond the bands in entry 1. EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S445, S452): 176 crores of accrual profit but only 16 crores of net operating cash flow over five years cannot fund a 300 to 400 crore capex from internal accruals, so equity dilution follows. S036: free cash flow is most reliable for mature businesses; a fast-growing company with heavy capex may show negative free cash flow without being low quality.
- **Quote:** "cash flow from operations turned negative because inventories increase drastically" (S348, AGROCG 00:14:43)
- **Sources:** 9 (S036, S043, S055, S160, S348, S445, S452, S453, S470)

### 3. Receivables: days against peers, spikes and contract assets
- **Check:** Compare receivable days with peers and with the company's own history, and read the receivable line against sales growth.
- **Detects:** Poor quality of sales, channel stuffing, and in the worst case sales that never turn into cash.
- **Red flag:** Receivable days disproportionately high versus peers, which the course says can mean fabricated sales, later exposed by large write-offs (S036); receivables and short-term debt rising together, which the course calls "automatic red flags" (S470); receivables growing with no provision (Caplin Point, S050); contract assets rising with no expected-credit-loss provision (S009); receivable days stretching, which the course calls poor sales quality (S057).
- **Green flag:** Receivable days flat or falling while sales grow. Cutting inventory and receivable days frees trapped capital and lifts CFO and ROCE (S036).
- **Where the data is:** Working-capital section of the annual report and the cash-flow statement. Receivable Days is a registry metric (S003).
- **Thresholds:**
  - RULE-style flag (S001): a spike in trade receivables "delayed by more than 180 days".
  - EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S258): receivable days of 140 to 150 on a subsidy-dependent business.
  - EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S010): receivable days rose only from 32 to 33 (FY23) to 42 (FY25), so a flagged rise was mild.
  - EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S050): Solara's Q2 receivables and inventory rose on channel stuffing, an exit signal before the MD resigned.
- **Quote:** "very very bad sign" (S050, MODULB 00:20:30)
- **Sources:** 9 (S001, S003, S009, S010, S036, S050, S057, S258, S470)

### 4. Legitimate exceptions the cash gate must respect
- **Check:** Before treating weak conversion as a red flag, ask whether the business model makes it normal.
- **Detects:** False positives from the cash screen.
- **Red flag:** Weak conversion with none of the exceptions below to explain it.
- **Green flag:** An explained exception, backed by debt-free status or a strong parent.
- **Where the data is:** Segment and business description in the annual report and concalls.
- **Thresholds:** None numeric. The exceptions the course names:
  - B2B models holding strategic inventory; B2G receivables stretched by the government; year-end inventory loading that reverses next half (S001).
  - Structural high inventory: basmati ages 12 to 24 months; gold retail is financed by gold-metal loans, so negative OCF is normal (S005). Jewellery cash flows nearly always look negative on paper (S304).
  - Negative working capital as a feature: Nestle debtor days about 4 and payable days about 112 (S007, PERISHABLE); city gas distribution has "high cash flow conversion because mostly negative working capital" (S308).
  - Agrochemicals carry high inventory, long receivable days and tight payable days, so poor CFO to EBITDA is a standing sector trait (S008, S341).
  - A negative-cash-flow B2G company can be held if it is debt-free or backed by a strong parent, such as Tejas Networks with Tata and TCS (S006, S232, PERISHABLE).
  - Early-stage companies can have weak CFO that improves (S160).
  - Lenders: the cash-flow gate does not apply (entry 41).
- **Quote:** "running on the basis of the money given by your suppliers" (S007)
- **Sources:** 10 (S001, S005, S006, S007, S008, S160, S232, S304, S308, S341)

### 5. One-offs booked in revenue, other income or exceptional items
- **Check:** Read the results footnotes for termination fees, compensation, write-offs and impairments, and strip them before reading growth.
- **Detects:** A headline jump that will not repeat, and a multiple that will de-rate once the anomaly passes.
- **Red flag:** Expected compensation or fees booked in revenue from operations or other income ahead of cash (SpiceJet and Boeing, S001, S055). One-offs also run the other way: in a turnaround, one-off costs or ESOP expense hide improving operations (S513, S520, S523, S528).
- **Green flag:** Growth that survives with the one-off removed.
- **Where the data is:** Results footnotes and key audit matters; the exceptional-items line.
- **Thresholds:**
  - EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S055): Aarti Industries' sequential jump from about 1500 to over 2300 crores included a contract termination fee of 631 crores; without it profit before tax growth was about 7 percent, not 50 to 60 percent.
  - EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S428): SJS Enterprises reported a 30.5 percent EBITDA margin but the adjusted margin was lower after a gratuity provision from the labour code.
  - EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S172): Jubilant's PAT growth reads as exceptional income; EBITDA growth was 23 percent while revenue growth was a little slow.
  - S037: lawsuit compensation, write-offs and goodwill impairment skew the bottom line and need manual adjustment.
- **Quote:** "inevitably guarantee a severe derating once the anomaly passes" (S001, HOWH 00:32:09)
- **Sources:** 9 (S001, S037, S055, S172, S428, S513, S520, S523, S528)

### 6. Normalised against reported PAT: needs a disclosed basis
- **Check:** Test any "normalised" or "adjusted" profit for a stated basis, and normalise cyclical margins to a long-run mean.
- **Detects:** Haircuts and add-backs that drive a whole valuation from one unaudited input, and peak-cycle earnings passing as normal.
- **Red flag:** Adjusted figures taken at face value (S005); an adjusted margin that differs from the reported one after a provision (S428); an adjusted EPS with no disclosed basis. S010 found a normalised EPS of 82.22 against reported 134.34 with no basis and no exceptional items in the filings; a DCF is linear in its starting EPS, so that one input drove the whole AVOID. Over-earning: Surya Roshni's API pipe EBITDA per ton rose from 4300 to 12134 (S319).
- **Green flag:** Adjustments traceable to a filed line.
- **Where the data is:** Filings and results footnotes; long-run margin history.
- **Thresholds:** EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S319): a P/E that looks like 15 to 17 times is more like 22 to 25 times after normalisation. Method (S087): model long-term average margins in valuation assumptions. The course states no haircut percentage.
- **Quote:** "Basically what happens when the margins get normalized is that the excess profitability that you have, goes away" (S319, UNCOVB 01:25:04)
- **Sources:** 5 (S005, S010, S087, S319, S428)

### 7. Volume against price: split growth into units and realisation
- **Check:** Break revenue growth into volume and realisation before trusting it.
- **Detects:** Growth that is a temporary price spike, and growth that is real.
- **Red flag:** Volumes up while realisations fall; a spike from higher realisations during a supply shock (Astec's Q4 FY22 jump came from Chinese lockdowns, with margins of 24 to 25 percent "abnormally elevated", S343); realisations that mean-revert (S008); hollow growth, "growth just for the sake of growth" (S348); "volumes grow but realizations fall" (S342); growth in volume that is not a real growth driver (S526).
- **Green flag:** Pure volume-led growth is the highest quality; value-led growth is good when it comes from a better mix (S039).
- **Where the data is:** Not on the income statement. Investor presentations and concalls (S039). HS-code export and import unit prices in a trade-data tool test whether realisation growth is real (S008).
- **Thresholds:** S002: F8 states a 10 percent volume-growth number, but value growth must not be substituted for volume growth. S320: the course wants volume growth of at least 8 to 15 percent for stable-margin converters (as spoken 10, 15, 8, 9). Industry units differ: liquor in cases of 12 bottles of 750 ml, hotels average revenue per room, hospitals average revenue per occupied bed, telecom average revenue per user (S039).
- **Quote:** "Volume is more important than value" (S039, MODULA 01:00:39)
- **Sources:** 8 (S002, S008, S039, S320, S342, S343, S348, S526)

### 8. Rate of change and base effect
- **Check:** Ask whether growth is accelerating or slowing, and what base it is measured from.
- **Detects:** The same headline growth meaning opposite things, and growth that is only a low or high base.
- **Red flag:** Decelerating growth behind a strong headline (smart meters against transmission EPC, S006); a high base next quarter ("high base effect", S501); a smaller base flattering growth (S284); rate of change as the core of fundamental analysis (S430); order books doubling off a large base compared with 150 percent off a small one (S006).
- **Green flag:** Margin expansion for three or four quarters, not one (S119).
- **Where the data is:** Quarterly results, year-ago comparison, order-book disclosure.
- **Thresholds:** RULE (S001 F25, S002): quarterly sales growth YoY at or above 15 and quarterly PAT growth YoY at or above 20. S119: demand three or four quarters of margin expansion; a strict filter can miss a good company that misses the 10 percent in one quarter.
- **Quote:** "accelerating or is the rate of change slowing now?" (S496, SOICE5 01:34:52)
- **Sources:** 8 (S001, S002, S006, S119, S284, S430, S496, S501)

### 9. Margin-source test: structural, or policy and cycle
- **Check:** Ask why the margin is fat and whether the cause survives its removal.
- **Detects:** Margins that rest on a tariff, subsidy or cycle rather than a moat.
- **Red flag:** Solar cells at 56 to 60 percent EBITDA only because of anti-dumping; a solar glass business earning 27 to 29 percent ROE for the same reason (S061); a market that exists only because of a subsidy (S258); "Without the subsidy, It's difficult to be cost competitive" (S218).
- **Green flag:** A margin that rests on IP trust and switching costs rather than a tariff (PI Industries passes this test, S009). Margin expansion without sales growth has a finite limit (S083). Management saying "margins are contracting because competition is increasing in the industry" shows a moat narrowing (S179).
- **Where the data is:** Policy documents, duty notifications, concalls.
- **Thresholds:** EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S005, S444): Borosil Renewals capacity 450 to 950 tons and La Opala RG earnings 13 crores to 59 crores under an anti-dumping regime. The course states no margin cut for this test.
- **Quote:** "Policy/cycle margins are NOT moats." (S005)
- **Sources:** 8 (S005, S009, S061, S083, S179, S218, S258, S444)

## 2. Accounting red flags

### 10. Expense capitalisation and depreciation changes
- **Check:** Read the disclosure of any change in useful life or depreciation method, and compare capitalised cost with how peers expense it.
- **Detects:** Profit lifted by moving cost to the balance sheet or cutting depreciation.
- **Red flag:** Capitalising R&D, which the course treats as aggressive; a depreciation cut that inflates EBIT and ROCE (S037); absurd ratios that give the game away.
- **Green flag:** Conservative companies expense R&D and pre-operating costs through the income statement; tech spend expensed upfront (S415).
- **Where the data is:** Key audit matters in the auditor's report; accounting-policy notes.
- **Thresholds:** The course states no numeric cut. S034: CWIP cannot be depreciated while the asset is under construction.
- **Quote:** "never know whether R&D will be fruitful or not" (S051, MODULC 00:39:13)
- **Sources:** 4 (S034, S037, S051, S415)

### 11. Goodwill, acquisitions and impairment assumptions
- **Check:** Look at acquisitions outside the core, the goodwill they create, and the assumptions used to avoid writing it down.
- **Detects:** Strategic drift and "kitchen sinking".
- **Red flag:** Too many acquisitions in non-core areas; raising expected operating margins from 5-15 percent to 5-20 percent, or using discount rates of 0.45 to 0.95 percent, to dodge a write-down (S053); a debt-fuelled acquisition spree that can drag a 30 percent return on capital to low single digits (S227); Indian companies buying European assets, called an orange flag (S006, S227) and "scared" (S194); "90% of MNAs fail" (S343, AGROCE 00:24:31).
- **Green flag:** An acquisition with a stated rationale, a consideration the company can fund, and execution by the promoter (S120). S467: acquisition is neutral by itself.
- **Where the data is:** Goodwill note and impairment-test note in the annual report.
- **Thresholds:** RULE (S053, 2026-07-27): a discount rate of 12 percent or higher is standard and terminal growth of 2 to 4 percent is historically normal; far more aggressive assumptions are the flag. S038 calls goodwill "fictitious asset, it is an imaginary asset, it exists in the imagination of the accountant".
- **Quote:** "known as kitchen sinking" (S053, MODULH 00:21:28)
- **Sources:** 8 (S006, S038, S053, S120, S194, S227, S343, S467)

### 12. Related-party transactions
- **Check:** Walk the annual report's related-party note line by line, and test that the transactions are genuine.
- **Detects:** Profit routed through connected entities, cash siphoned to promoter subsidiaries, earnings smoothing.
- **Red flag:** Commission rates that vary to smooth earnings or margins; loans or advances to promoter entities where the listed firm holds a minority stake; subsidiaries audited by secondary auditors (S054); transfer pricing and related-party lending in agrochemicals (S005, S008); a slump sale with "too many related party transactions" (S354); Timex royalty and related-party governance to track (S126); a spot seller with criminal cases and related-party transactions (S345); Poshak failing the course filters on poor disclosure and related-party transactions (S422); heavy guarantees and advances to a group (Inox Group, S364); royalty pushed up while scaling (Timex, S132); notes that span many pages and are "very difficult to verify" (S470).
- **Green flag:** Healthy OCF-to-EBITDA conversion, debtor days showing money is collected, and dividends paid (S054).
- **Where the data is:** Related-party note, borrowings note, corporate guarantees, directors' interests.
- **Thresholds:**
  - RULE (S066, 2026-07-27): related-party transactions above 10 percent deserve suspicion. S068: above 10 percent of sales.
  - RULE (S068): routing 35 to 40 percent of PAT through related parties makes a company generally considered uninvestable.
  - S054 states no numeric cut for its genuineness tests.
  - Complex structures with excessive related-party transactions are an amber flag (S001, S456).
- **Quote:** "Anything more than 10% should be viewed with suspicion." (S066, MODULJ 00:11:24)
- **Sources:** 14 (S001, S005, S008, S054, S066, S068, S126, S132, S345, S354, S364, S422, S456, S470)

### 13. Auditor and key-management resignations
- **Check:** Track resignations of the statutory auditor, CFO, MD and independent directors over several years.
- **Detects:** Governance stress, often before numbers show it.
- **Red flag:** A mass exodus is an amber flag that demands skepticism, not proof of fraud (S052); repeated governance lapses, auditor departure or the whole board resigning, which the course treats as exit triggers (S111); a CFO resigning three times in a year plus an auditor resignation, "very risky" and not a speed breaker (S116); mass CFO or board resignations shortly after an IPO (S068); a CEO exit and three CFOs over FY21 to FY25 (S009, PERISHABLE); waves of KMP and independent-director resignations (S005); sudden exits of key managers (S343); an MD resigning after channel stuffing (Solara, S050).
- **Green flag:** Stable CFO and senior directors over a decade (S298).
- **Where the data is:** Exchange filings; annual report; the management-change history.
- **Thresholds:** The course states no count. EXAMPLE (S052): statutory auditor resignations spiked to 42 across the market in 2018 (PERISHABLE). S052: do not average down on a falling stock during such events; catastrophic losses followed for those who did. Ignoring mass resignations or an RBI refusal of an MD extension is called "cardinal sins".
- **Quote:** "very risky" (S116, FRAMEA 00:48:05)
- **Sources:** 9 (S005, S009, S050, S052, S068, S111, S116, S298, S343)

### 14. Contingent liabilities and tax disputes
- **Check:** Read the contingent-liability note as a share of net worth, and separate normal items from the ones that bite.
- **Detects:** Off-balance-sheet claims, mostly guarantees to relatives and subsidiaries.
- **Red flag:** Guarantees for related parties; open tax demands that are large against net worth.
- **Green flag:** Letters of credit, performance guarantees and export guarantees, which are normal (S049); a flagged tax demand later won on appeal (S010, stale flag).
- **Where the data is:** Contingent-liability note; tax-dispute disclosure.
- **Thresholds:**
  - RULE (S049, 2026-07-27): be cautious if contingent liabilities exceed 20 percent of net worth.
  - EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S049): one company at 479 percent of net worth, mostly guarantees to relatives and subsidiaries. S008: Heranba at 61 percent, PERISHABLE.
  - S456: severe criminal cases or frauds mean instant rejection; routine tax disputes may be tolerated since nearly all top 500 companies have contingent tax liabilities.
  - EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S444): Delta Corp faced a tax demand of roughly 16,000 crores.
  - S313, S182: retrospective taxation and windfall taxes are regional risks the course names.
- **Quote:** "normal part of the business" (S049, MODULG 00:26:09)
- **Sources:** 7 (S008, S010, S049, S182, S313, S444, S456)

### 15. Annual-report line items: miscellaneous expenses, remuneration, employee cost
- **Check:** Read the expense schedule and the remuneration disclosure for lines that are too big or do not fit the story.
- **Detects:** Catch-all expense lines hiding spend, over-paid management, and capacity claims that do not show in staffing.
- **Red flag:** A miscellaneous line that is a large share of other expenses; a basic-versus-diluted EPS gap, which flags heavy ESOP dilution (S060); management boasting expansion while employee cost as a percent of sales stays flat ("all these things are going on in the air", S045); absurd ratios, such as "20 lakhs of computers per employee" (S051, MODULE 00:24:13); promoters with unlisted companies in a similar line of business, or directors with criminal or tax cases (S066, S071).
- **Green flag:** Delivery of what was promised ("whether they are walking the talk or not", S066).
- **Where the data is:** Annual report schedules; DRHP for independent board credentials (S026).
- **Thresholds:**
  - RULE (S066, 2026-07-27): miscellaneous expenses at 20 to 30 percent of total other expenses are a red flag.
  - RULE (S066): the maximum remuneration a company can normally take is 11 percent of PAT.
  - S060: a negative change in inventories must be subtracted from raw materials.
- **Quote:** "if it represents 20-30% of total other expenses, it constitutes a red flag" (S066, MODULJ 00:06:21)
- **Sources:** 6 (S026, S045, S051, S060, S066, S071)

### 16. Pre-IPO window dressing and the first post-IPO base
- **Check:** Compare the first post-IPO quarters with a base that may have been lifted before listing.
- **Detects:** A margin spike before the sale that makes later growth look weak, or an IPO sold at a peak.
- **Red flag:** Promoters selling at the peak: "promoters want to secure a high valuation when selling their shares at the peak" (S507); mass CFO or board resignations shortly after the IPO (S068).
- **Green flag:** An earnings surprise with positive forward guidance after the base has normalised (S507). S053: give a newly listed company time to prove itself.
- **Where the data is:** DRHP and the first four results after listing.
- **Thresholds:** The course states these as a pattern, not a rule (S507, 2026-07-27). The first 3 to 4 quarters after listing usually show growth struggles because the prior-year base was artificially elevated. After about 1.5 to 2 years the stock goes sideways for roughly 9 to 12 months. Bombed-out phase 15 to 24 months, then 9 to 12 months of bottoming. Earnings downgrade cycles 4 to 8 quarters, sometimes up to 4 years.
- **Quote:** "give a company some time to prove itself" (S053, MODULH 00:11:58)
- **Sources:** 3 (S053, S068, S507)

### 17. Revenue recognition method and timing
- **Check:** Find how revenue is recognised and whether that makes quarters uneven or flatters a year.
- **Detects:** Lumpy or front-loaded profit that is not a trend.
- **Red flag:** Subjectivity under the old real-estate method ("a lot of subjectivity", S371); inventory-to-sales high, meaning "project is stuck" (S371); quarters made uneven by "project completion method" (S217); revenue and fees booked ahead of cash (S055).
- **Green flag:** Customer advances rising, an "indication that the builder is seeing increased sales" (S371).
- **Where the data is:** Revenue-recognition policy note; real estate: customer advances, equivalent area constructed, inventory to sales.
- **Thresholds:** The course states no numeric cut. S371: revenue "gets lumpy as units tend to be delivered in batches". S374: "The profit we see today is that of the project 3 years ago". S446: real estate cannot use EV/EBITDA; use NAV or project cash flows. S382: for life insurers, up-fronting of expenses for revenues accrued over the next 20 years substantially understates accounting profit.
- **Quote:** "Revenue recognized gets lumpy as units tend to be delivered in batches" (S371, REAL 00:49:22)
- **Sources:** 6 (S055, S217, S371, S374, S382, S446)

## 3. Balance sheet and leverage

### 18. Debt to equity and what counts as debt
- **Check:** Compute debt to equity and read what is inside it, including leases and negative reserves.
- **Detects:** Stretched balance sheets and equity wiped out by losses.
- **Red flag:** Repeated losses pushing retained earnings and other equity negative and debt to equity very high (SpiceJet at 15 to 16 times, S035, PERISHABLE); debt ballooning against negative reserves, "good money after bad money" (S001, S451); debt "always on the higher side with the equity" (S161, PERISHABLE).
- **Green flag:** Deleveraging toward zero long-term debt (S001, S451). Near-zero debt "won't help them in up cycle" and "down cycle it won't destroy them also" (S325, hotels).
- **Where the data is:** Balance sheet equity and borrowings; lease liabilities after Ind AS 116.
- **Thresholds:**
  - RULE (S001 F27, S002, S451): debt to equity at or below 0.7 is the healthy level; S451 says "less than 0.7 times for a healthy balance sheet".
  - RULE (S001 F26, S456): debt to equity above 1 is treated with caution.
  - Ind AS 116 lease liabilities now count as debt, so the ratio "will start looking higher" (S038).
  - The course states no lender-specific cut; S332 describes PSU and regional bank leverage of 14 to 16 times as part of the sector (PERISHABLE).
- **Quote:** "debt-to-equity ratio of less than 0.7 times for a healthy balance sheet" (S451, MASTEA 01:52:15)
- **Sources:** 9 (S001, S002, S035, S038, S161, S325, S332, S451, S456)

### 19. Debt-funded expansion bigger than the balance sheet
- **Check:** Compare the size of announced capex with existing fixed assets and net worth, and ask who funds it.
- **Detects:** Bet-the-company expansion and capacity that arrives into a downturn.
- **Red flag:** Capex worth seven times net worth followed by an industry slowdown, ending in bankruptcy (S034, EXAMPLE); "quadrupling its fixed asset base with a 2,200-crore investment" (S252, PERISHABLE); "bet the farm type of bet" (S465); a plant "doubling the size of the nearly balanced sheet" (S136); growth funded by very heavy debt, such as a revenue climb from $15 million in 2021 to over $5 billion (S459, PERISHABLE); debt-funded capex that fails to commercialise on time (S444); fixed assets funded entirely by long-term debt (S451); "over leveraged" after a downturn (S453).
- **Green flag:** Capex funded from internal cash flow, with debt falling.
- **Where the data is:** CWIP and fixed assets; borrowings; capex guidance in concalls.
- **Thresholds:** The course states no ratio. S252: capacity "doesn't necessarily mean that the company will do well". S231: scale funded by debt with weak cash flow is "structurally fragile".
- **Quote:** "bet the farm type of bet" (S465, SOICB2 01:05:21)
- **Sources:** 9 (S034, S136, S231, S252, S444, S451, S453, S459, S465)

### 20. Deleveraging claims: interest, borrowings and repayment must agree
- **Check:** When management says it is cutting debt, confirm that interest cost, borrowings and a debt-repayment outflow all moved the same way (S445).
- **Detects:** A deleveraging story that the statements do not support, and profit growth that is only lower interest.
- **Red flag:** A claim with no repayment outflow in the cash flow; debt cut by asset sales or capital infusion without asking why it needed cutting (S513, S523).
- **Green flag:** Long-term debt falling toward zero (S001, S451); interest costs going down on the profit and loss statement (S429); lenders converting debt to equity or a company paying down massive debt, which can show in the balance sheet before the income statement (S035).
- **Where the data is:** Interest line, borrowings, cash-flow financing section.
- **Thresholds:** EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S325): Samhi's interest cost fell from 13 percent to 10.2 percent and net debt to EBITDA from 8.7 times pre-IPO toward 5.1 times, guided to 3.5 times.
- **Quote:** "the balance sheet can be fixed ahead of, or in parallel with, the P&L recovery" (S527)
- **Sources:** 9 (S001, S035, S325, S429, S445, S451, S513, S523, S527)

### 21. Equity dilution, rights, QIPs and buybacks
- **Check:** Track share capital and reserves across years, and who takes part in a rights issue, warrant or buyback.
- **Detects:** Dilution funding a cash-poor business, and buybacks that destroy value at a peak.
- **Red flag:** Frequent dilution to fund operations that do not generate cash (S452); a buyback at peak multiples; a buyback with the promoter not tendering, rare in a micro-cap (S155, PERISHABLE); a large QIP raised for a purpose not executed (S343, PERISHABLE); a rights issue by a company going through a turnaround (HCC, S154, PERISHABLE).
- **Green flag:** A buyback that lowers the share count and lifts EPS and ROE (S445); promoters taking part in a rights issue or putting money in through warrants, a "bullish sign" (S035). A buyback done "just at a reasonable valuation" creates value (S035).
- **Where the data is:** Share capital and reserves note; basic versus diluted EPS (S060); exchange filings.
- **Thresholds:** EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S035): Apple cut its share-capital count by 35 percent over eight years. The course states no dilution cut.
- **Quote:** "bullish sign that the promoter is willing to put in more money into the company" (S035, MODULC 01:18:45)
- **Sources:** 7 (S035, S060, S154, S155, S343, S445, S452)

### 22. Idle cash and capital misallocation
- **Check:** Compare cash and investments with total assets, and ask where the cash goes.
- **Detects:** Cash that lowers ROE, or cash that leaves through promoter subsidiaries.
- **Red flag:** A market cap below cash that tempts Graham-style buyers but where the cash is often siphoned to promoter subsidiaries; capex that shows no later sales growth ("zero sales growth", S049); management hoarding cash or misallocating capital instead of distributing it (S026); hoarded cash depressing ROE (S061).
- **Green flag:** Cash returned through dividends and buybacks at reasonable valuations.
- **Where the data is:** Balance sheet; capex against later sales; dividend history.
- **Thresholds:** EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S445): 1300 crores of 2735 crores total assets in cash and investments depresses ROE. S456: hoarding cash without dividends is an amber flag.
- **Quote:** "Benjamin Graham thinking" (S049, MODULG 00:02:31)
- **Sources:** 5 (S026, S049, S061, S445, S456)

### 23. Asset intensity, leases and the limits of EBITDA
- **Check:** Decide whether EBITDA is the right yardstick for a lease-heavy or asset-heavy business.
- **Detects:** EBITDA that flatters poor cash converters or hides depreciation and lease cost.
- **Red flag:** Using EBITDA margins for airlines, retailers or quick-service restaurants; depreciation that distorts capital-heavy sectors (S228).
- **Green flag:** Peer comparison on PAT margin for lease-heavy businesses; an exit market cap to EBITDA multiple for asset-heavy ones (S042).
- **Where the data is:** Lease note; depreciation and lease rentals against sales.
- **Thresholds:** RULE-style (S038): for lease-heavy businesses focus on PAT margin. EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S001 F24): an asset-light platform may lose about 3.6 to 3.7 percent of sales to depreciation against about 20 percent for an asset-heavy telecom or hospital operator. S228: choose the multiple by cash conversion; "These ratios are rough valuation exercises."
- **Quote:** "null and void for us as investors" (S038, MODULD 01:16:46)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S038, S042, S228)

## 4. Working capital and activity ratios

### 24. The working-capital cycle and its link to ROCE
- **Check:** Track inventory days, receivable days and payable days together, against peers.
- **Detects:** Capital trapped in the cycle and rising short-term debt.
- **Red flag:** Rising working-capital days as a "very very bad sign" of sales quality (S050); raw-material spikes bloating inventory and receivables and pushing short-term debt up (S451); high working capital with low margin, the "lethal combination" (S063); financing loans slipped into working capital under operating activities, found in footnotes (S050).
- **Green flag:** A shorter cycle that lowers short-term debt and capital employed and lifts ROCE and free cash flow (S057); a cash-and-carry model, as APL Apollo moved to (S036, and S058 for Divi's Labs margins, APL Apollo and DMart turnover, PERISHABLE); lower inventory or debtor days freeing cash (S452); negligible working capital with extreme turns, as at exchanges (S063).
- **Where the data is:** Working-capital section of the annual report.
- **Thresholds:** EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S050): Hatsun held 72 to 75 percent conversion for four years while Kwality and Parag Milk Foods fell and turned negative. S057: payable turnover is ambiguous; it can mean "higher bargaining power over its suppliers".
- **Quote:** "balance sheet is a greater reality than what a profit and loss statement would imply" (S058, MODULH 02:03:49)
- **Sources:** 7 (S036, S050, S057, S058, S063, S451, S452)

### 25. Working-capital intensity and slow government payers
- **Check:** Ask how long the customer takes to pay, and whether the customer is a government body.
- **Detects:** Profit that is given away in working capital, and orders that cannot be funded.
- **Red flag:** "what they make in profits is they give away in working capital" (S215, PSU customers, cycles of 400 to 500 days, PERISHABLE); "I've heard stories, painful stories that you are not getting money for 15-15 months, they are not getting money for 18-18 months" (S166); government payment stress of 15 to 18 months (S481, PERISHABLE); "orders will also come but there is no working capital" in water and smart meters (S184); payment "coming from the state government so this B2G problem is happening" (S145); power-sector mix keeping working capital "elevated" (S165); about 40 percent of domestic sales from B2G, "that will always be a risk" (S161, PERISHABLE).
- **Green flag:** Working capital elevated only because the company is early in its growth (Scoda, S166).
- **Where the data is:** Receivables and inventory days; customer mix in concalls.
- **Thresholds:** No cut. EXAMPLE (S214, PERISHABLE): shipping is a "very high working capital industry" at 400 to 500 days. S341, S347: agrochemical technical grades are very high working-capital businesses.
- **Quote:** "what they make in profits is they give away in working capital" (S215, FUTURA 00:44:30)
- **Sources:** 10 (S145, S161, S165, S166, S184, S214, S215, S341, S347, S481)

### 26. Inventory gains and losses on commodity swings
- **Check:** Ask how the margin moves when the raw-material price moves, and whether the inventory is hedged.
- **Detects:** Margin that is an inventory gain, and a coming loss.
- **Red flag:** Stocking before a price crash; no hedge ("a company might post a 12% margin in one quarter and 4% the next", S253); unhedged gold inventory inflating ROCE in rising gold (S005); a high import price of an input predicting inventory losses (S008); inventory losses when high-priced stock sells into a falling market (S037, S348); inventory gains lifting realisation (S037, S483).
- **Green flag:** Hedged inventory. PNG Gadgil's store ROC reflects only 35 percent inventory hedged against 90 percent plus for peers, which the course calls unrewarded risk (S007, PERISHABLE).
- **Where the data is:** Gross margin, inventory days, raw-material price series.
- **Thresholds:** EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S280): after stocking at a high price, raw material crashed 80 percent and the company took inventory losses for 4 to 8 quarters; margin went from 22 percent to 6 percent.
- **Quote:** "the stock was at a high price and at the end of 6 months the prices of raw material crashed 80% so they had to take huge inventory losses for 4 to 8 quarters" (S280, AROMA 00:35:15)
- **Sources:** 8 (S005, S007, S008, S037, S253, S280, S348, S483)

### 27. Asset turnover and fixed-asset intensity
- **Check:** Read net fixed-asset turnover against sector norms, and watch it during a capex phase.
- **Detects:** Asset-heavy against asset-light economics, and capacity not yet earning.
- **Red flag:** Asset turns that "go for a toss" after integration (S363, S367); integrated makers showing higher margin per ton but lower asset turnover (S321); asset turns that stay low after capex.
- **Green flag:** Turns that fall first then recover; asset-light businesses that often command premium valuations (S451).
- **Where the data is:** Fixed assets against sales.
- **Thresholds:** S290: margins of 8 to 11 percent sit with high asset turns in pre-engineered buildings. EXAMPLE sector norms (S062): net fixed-asset turns 1 to 1.5 times for pharma but high in agro; specialty chemicals operating margins above 15 to 18 percent with net fixed-asset turnover under 2 times. EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S403): Laurus asset turns fell 2017 to 2019 and returned to 2.3 times; Syngene gross fixed-asset turns fell to 0.6 times from close to 1 time. S405: asset turns near 3 times like agrochemicals.
- **Quote:** "challenge of lower asset turns is front-ended, but the opportunity of higher sales and sweating out your assets is backended" (S403, APIK 00:10:20)
- **Sources:** 8 (S062, S290, S321, S363, S367, S403, S405, S451)

## 5. Return ratios and DuPont

### 28. ROE through DuPont: margin, turnover, leverage
- **Check:** Split ROE into net margin, asset turnover and leverage, and see which one is doing the work.
- **Detects:** Return that is borrowed, or return that is dragged down by hoarded cash or fresh equity.
- **Red flag:** Leverage's share rising while margin and turns flatten (the Tata Motors shape, S001, PERISHABLE); equity dilution through a QIP cutting ROE; hoarded cash depressing it (S061); a bank with single-digit ROE (S359).
- **Green flag:** ROE driven by margin and turnover rather than debt (S061). Vedant Fashions at 28 percent ROE on about a 31 percent net margin; APL Apollo at about 24.64 percent ROE with turnover near three times (both PERISHABLE).
- **Where the data is:** Net profit, sales, total assets and net worth; the screener ratio grid carries ROE and ROCE.
- **Thresholds:**
  - RULE (S001 F26, S456): ROC or ROE above 15 percent, or able to cross it.
  - Context (S061, 2026-07-27): India's cost of capital about 10 percent; ROE of 25 percent is "phenomenal"; the Nifty 500 average ROE is about 14 percent. S087: Nifty long-term earnings growth and ROE between 12 and 14 percent. S084: cost of capital "generally estimated to be around eight to ten percent". S359: for banks, "at least double digit return on equity".
  - The course does not give a DuPont cut for leverage share.
- **Quote:** "excessive debt can cause the ROE to rapidly collapse or turn negative" (S001, MODULE 00:18:16)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S061, S084, S087, S359, S456)

### 29. ROE and ROCE at face value mislead in cycles and on old plants
- **Check:** Ask whether a high return reflects a cycle peak, a fully depreciated plant, inventory gains or a tariff.
- **Detects:** Return ratios that will not last.
- **Red flag:** ROE at peaks and troughs (CEAT's ROE fell from peak double digits to about 6 percent on raw-material cost, PERISHABLE); fully depreciated plants inflating ROCE (KPR at 30 to 35 percent, S005, PERISHABLE); unhedged inventory gains inflating ROCE (S005); a long flat chart as a heuristic for poor return on capital (S032); rising EPS with falling ROCE, which the course says degrades earnings quality and signals a value trap (S058).
- **Green flag:** Return that persists through the cycle on a growing capital base.
- **Where the data is:** ROCE series over a full cycle; depreciation schedule.
- **Thresholds:** EXAMPLE sector ranges (PERISHABLE): fully integrated manufacturers with depreciated plants reach ROCE of 24 to 35 percent while traditional garmenting is capped at 15 to 17 percent and "single digit ROC" in a downturn (S306).
- **Quote:** "highly deceptive in cyclical industries" (S001, MODULH 00:50:23)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S005, S032, S058, S306)

### 30. Incremental returns and reinvestment
- **Check:** Ask what return new capital earns and whether there is room to reinvest it.
- **Detects:** Legacy moats that earn well but cannot deploy more capital, and growth that is bought with low-return capex.
- **Red flag:** No ongoing capex in a manufacturing business (S084); margin expansion without sales growth, which has a finite limit (S083); a QIP inflating capital and giving a "skewed picture" (S059).
- **Green flag:** Employing "large amounts of incremental capital at very high rates of return" (S059); a moat that only helps if it keeps reinvesting (S081); capex falling while ROC rises (S351).
- **Where the data is:** Change in operating profit against change in capital employed over several years.
- **Thresholds:** The course does not give an incremental-return cut. EXAMPLE (S082): a 6 percent ROC over 40 years gives about 6 percent to the investor; sustaining 18 percent over 20 to 30 years creates large wealth; in the S&P 500 from the 1910s to the 2000s, 4 percent of businesses generated more than 99 percent of wealth. S080: returns above 50 percent are "obscene returns on capital".
- **Quote:** "Capital multiplies faster than redeployment opportunities." (S084, MODULC 00:05:48)
- **Sources:** 7 (S059, S080, S081, S082, S083, S084, S351)

### 31. The margin ladder: gross, operating, and what moved
- **Check:** Read gross margin for mix and pricing power, and operating margin for utilisation and fixed-cost leverage.
- **Detects:** Lost pricing power, a real mix shift, or operating deleverage.
- **Red flag:** A fall in gross margin (EXAMPLE 47 percent to 42 percent shows lost pricing power, S060); operating deleverage when capex and staff cost rise without sales (S037); highly volatile margins, which often warrant avoiding the business (S456); a mix shift that "might not pay out as expected" (S110).
- **Green flag:** Gross margin expansion from a better mix; a new segment growing faster than legacy at higher margin; margin expansion for three or four quarters (S119).
- **Where the data is:** Income statement; yearly operating margin is where a mix shift first shows (S110).
- **Thresholds:**
  - RULE (S001 F25 addition, 2026-07-28): operating-margin expansion of 10 percent quarter over quarter, market cap under 20,000cr, breakout above 30 percent over a flat baseline, OPM above a 1 percent floor.
  - S170: gross margin is mix and operating margin is utilisation. EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE: about 55 percent gross margin with full utilisation gives about 25 percent operating margin.
  - S060: PBT margin uses total income, operations plus other income, as denominator.
- **Quote:** "structural mix shift might not pay out as expected" (S110, FRAMEB 00:47:03)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S037, S060, S110, S119, S170, S456)

### 32. Segment and division level: look below the consolidated number
- **Check:** Split profit and return by segment and by division.
- **Detects:** A weak large segment hiding behind one strong small one, or a consolidated margin masking acquisition losses.
- **Red flag:** ITC's FMCG at barely a 6 percent margin on 16,000 crores of sales while cigarettes drive profit (S040, PERISHABLE); Gland's consolidated margin falling on losses from a European acquisition (S251, PERISHABLE); a parent whose consolidated P/E is "very, very wrong metric to look at" because minority-investment losses consolidate (S413).
- **Green flag:** A division growing steadily: Gujarat Ambuja Exports' maize grew from 401 crores to 2,644 crores over a decade while cotton yarn lost money (S040, PERISHABLE).
- **Where the data is:** Segment note in the annual report; use sum-of-the-parts for a holding structure.
- **Thresholds:** S040: annualising one quarter by multiplying by four assumes the segment stays this profitable all year. S197 (EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE): a hydraulics business with 9 to 20 percent ROC across its cycle.
- **Quote:** "very, very wrong metric to look at" (S413, MODERF 00:25:53)
- **Sources:** 4 (S040, S197, S251, S413)

## 6. Promoter, pledge, governance and shareholding

### 33. Promoter pledge
- **Check:** Read promoter pledging beside receivables, debt and cash flow.
- **Detects:** Promoter financing stress that may spill into the company.
- **Red flag:** Rising pledge alongside rising receivables and debt, called "automatic red flags" (S001, S470); fully pledged promoter shares, a "huge red flag" (S470); promoter pledges, excessive intercompany loans, and repeatedly delayed or diluted announcements (S113).
- **Green flag:** Screens that avoid "pledged companies or those with negative cash flows" (S259).
- **Where the data is:** Shareholding pattern; annual report.
- **Thresholds:** The course does not give a pledge percentage cut in these sources. The strongest statement is fully pledged. S470 adds that a promoter on TV frequently is only a "softer red flag".
- **Quote:** "avoiding pledged companies or those with negative cash flows" (S259, CAPITC 00:30:03)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S113, S259, S470)

### 34. Promoter buying, selling, warrants and incentives
- **Check:** Track promoter stake changes, warrants and pay structure against the business cycle and guidance.
- **Detects:** Alignment, or an exit into strength.
- **Red flag:** A promoter who gives bullish guidance but sells during headwinds (S456); insider selling into bullish guidance, a hard red flag (S001); heavy promoter selling or joining buybacks near a cycle peak (S068); pay tied to market cap, which can lead to "fudging of numbers" (S434); promoter group selling at Timex (S245, PERISHABLE).
- **Green flag:** Warrants at a premium to fund a plant (APL Apollo, 25 to 30 percent premium, S467, PERISHABLE); promoter buying 4 to 5 years after selling at the top (Shivalik Bi-Metal, S155, PERISHABLE); rising stake backed by real expansion (S434); promoters joining a rights issue or warrants (S035); promoter buying at Kilburn (S158, PERISHABLE).
- **Where the data is:** Shareholding pattern; warrant and buyback filings.
- **Thresholds:** S434: a rising stake is a stronger signal when backed by expansion, such as fixed assets and CWIP doubling while capacity triples; that is a course example, not a hard cutoff. EXAMPLE (S434): promoter holding as low as 2 to 7 percent after ESOPs is a weak-incentive pattern. The course states no stake-change cut.
- **Quote:** "to show confidence in the company and to infuse more money" (S467, SOICD3 01:06:17)
- **Sources:** 9 (S001, S035, S068, S155, S158, S245, S434, S456, S467)

### 35. Promoter background, integrity and group governance
- **Check:** Look at the promoter's past, the group's record and the directors' cases.
- **Detects:** Governance risk that a clean year hides.
- **Red flag:** A principal-agent conflict, with management hoarding cash or misallocating capital (S026); PSU incentives that differ from minority shareholders (S026); a promoter with a poor past (S225, S236, S373); "average to bad governance because of high RTPs" (S364); a group "not a group which has had highest standards of corporate governance" (S260); directors with criminal litigation or tax disputes (S071). Cheap does not remove the need for triggers (S298).
- **Green flag:** An independent background check that finds nothing; stable promoters and leadership.
- **Where the data is:** DRHP, annual report, litigation disclosure, news history.
- **Thresholds:** RULE-style (S001 F26, S456): severe fraud or criminal cases are an instant reject; routine tax disputes are tolerable; amber flags are cash hoarding without dividends and complex structures with heavy related-party transactions. The course states that it did not check promoter integrity for one sector list: "I don't have Zero clue about the promoter integrity because of the past" (S207).
- **Quote:** "But you have to be aware of the promoter's past also, so that is something that you have to be careful of" (S225, ALC 00:24:11)
- **Sources:** 11 (S001, S026, S071, S207, S225, S236, S260, S298, S364, S373, S456)

### 36. Hidden promoter holdings and free float
- **Check:** Test whether "public" holders are really the promoter's, and whether a thin float is distorting the price.
- **Detects:** Promoter control through proxies and a price set by a cornered float.
- **Red flag:** Promoters holding "through their own cronies and proxies" (S357); public holders with identical registration addresses (Adani Green, S357, PERISHABLE); about 12 percent of a float held by long-standing brokers suspected, unconfirmed, as promoter proxies (Bharat Rasayan, S008, PERISHABLE); a cornered float where "you won't be able to make sense of the valuations ever" (S361).
- **Green flag:** A low float that sits with real earnings; low float alone is not enough because "there is no earnings in the business" (S357).
- **Where the data is:** Shareholding pattern; list of public holders above 1 percent.
- **Thresholds:** EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S357): Bharat Rasayan promoters officially 75 percent but more than 86 percent through interconnected brokers. Stated in a lecture on one company's raise (S171, PERISHABLE): after listing a promoter has 36 months to reach the SEBI maximum permissible holding of 75 percent. S484: the marginal buyer in small caps is usually PMS, AIF, family offices or retail, not mutual funds.
- **Quote:** "through their own cronies and proxies" (S357, MASTEB 00:00:19)
- **Sources:** 5 (S008, S171, S357, S361, S484)

### 37. Institutional sponsorship
- **Check:** Watch whether public shareholding falls while FII and DII holding rises, and for how long.
- **Detects:** Smart-money accumulation, and its limits as a signal.
- **Red flag:** Institutions selling while the public share rises (S123); copying institutions when the industry is in stage 4; a thin base of institutional holders that leaves "12 to 18 months" of sellers (S281).
- **Green flag:** Retail share falling while FII plus DII share rises (S123). Retail cannot move market caps of 4,000 to 5,000 crores, so large volume on up days is a footprint (S100, S115). Heavy DII and FII holdings tend to sit where valuation is high (S359).
- **Where the data is:** Quarterly shareholding pattern.
- **Thresholds:** RULE-style (S001 F33): FII plus DII combined stake rising 2 percentage points or more in a year is a screen input. The course's own backtest of 54 businesses found only 25 outperforming, about a coin flip (S467, S001). S123: do not follow FII and DII blindly; check the market cycle and industry stage.
- **Quote:** "only 25 business are outperforming" (S467, SOICE3 01:01:28)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S100, S115, S123, S281, S359, S467)

## 7. Cash flow and capex

### 38. Capex that brings no revenue
- **Check:** Test whether past capex shows up as later sales and earnings, and whether new capacity is used.
- **Detects:** Money "put in the drain", and depreciation and running cost landing before sales.
- **Red flag:** New capacity not utilised, so depreciation and running costs (power, salaries) hit while sales stay flat and profits erode into losses (S034); capex with "zero sales growth" (S049); capacity added in a down cycle (S449); "missed allocation" (S230); announced capex that never turns into execution on the ground (S257); a "big capex" mindset that waits for return ratios to follow (S198).
- **Green flag:** Sales growth that outpaces the multiplying expenses (S034); unutilised fixed assets in a turning cycle, an "operating leverage time bomb" (S449).
- **Where the data is:** CWIP and fixed assets on the balance sheet; depreciation and employee cost on the income statement.
- **Thresholds:** The course states no numeric cut. EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S180): "2016's Kpex started, now it's 2026", with little revenue to show. S084: no ongoing capex in manufacturing is a warning.
- **Quote:** "2016's Kpex started, now it's 2026. Now the revenue generation, I just think they've just put the money in the drain." (S180, MOATB 01:12:02)
- **Sources:** 8 (S034, S049, S084, S180, S198, S230, S257, S449)

### 39. CWIP, commissioning and utilisation
- **Check:** Read CWIP against fixed assets to see how near capacity is to going live, then track utilisation after it does.
- **Detects:** An operating-profit step that is coming, or a ramp that is slow.
- **Red flag:** Greenfield spend in a new city, "the most painful" (S351); a 1 to 1.5 year pre-operational expense phase (S251) or 10 to 12 months before the high-margin product starts (S253).
- **Green flag:** A high CWIP-to-fixed-assets ratio near completion, which the market often rewards ahead of the growth (S034); capex starting to decline while ROC expands (S351); brownfield "within existing area" the most lucrative (S351).
- **Where the data is:** Balance sheet CWIP and fixed assets; concall commissioning guidance; for E&P compare CWIP with the current net block (S315).
- **Thresholds:** S034 gives no CWIP-to-fixed-assets threshold. RULE-style, two schools (S112, 2026-07-27): buy at the worst pre-opex quarters, or wait for a de-risked entry at capacity utilisation of "30-40%". It states these as two approaches, not one rule. Not for lenders (S034).
- **Quote:** "Now KPEX starts declining. Your ROC starts expanding" (S351, SOICB 00:06:23)
- **Sources:** 6 (S034, S112, S251, S253, S315, S351)

### 40. Order-book quality
- **Check:** Ask whether the order book is growing or shrinking, recurring or one-off, funded, and convertible.
- **Detects:** A story that has run ahead of conversion, and one that is running out.
- **Red flag:** An order book that "has degrown year on year" or equals one year of revenue (S155); "minus 87% orders have been fulfilled we don't have any new order" (S232); a pipeline treated as a story until orders convert (S158); a technology claim with no orders behind it (S207); order-book themes with little terminal value (S256, S476); a high base effect (S501); an order book near 90 percent of forecast revenue as a cover reading (S239, PERISHABLE).
- **Green flag:** Orders that repeat (81 percent repeat orders in FY24 at one PEB maker, S288, PERISHABLE); a company that discloses its order book monthly (S161).
- **Where the data is:** Concalls and order announcements; arithmetic from megawatts or units times revenue per unit (S155).
- **Thresholds:** EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S155): 2,832 MW at about Rs. 1.2 crore a megawatt gives about Rs. 3,300 to 3,400 crore against actual sales of Rs. 3,321 crore. S519: short-cycle orders scale faster than multi-year locked ones; S517: execution takes six to nine months, or three to four years for road construction firms. S211 (PERISHABLE): IdeaForge's order book is given as 137 crores plus a 400 crore pipeline, and later as 145 crores with 400 crores in L1; the two are not reconciled.
- **Quote:** "order book stories are the worst stories to sit through when the order book is contracting" (S112, FRAMEB 00:25:10)
- **Sources:** 14 (S112, S155, S158, S161, S207, S211, S232, S239, S256, S288, S476, S501, S517, S519)

## 8. Lenders and insurers

### 41. Banks and NBFCs: the ROA tree, credit cost and price to book
- **Check:** For a lender, replace the cash-flow gate with the ROA tree: net interest margin, fee income, treasury income, operating cost and credit cost. Read price to book only with ROE, ROA, GNPA and NNPA.
- **Detects:** A "cheap" bank that is cheap for a reason, and margin bought with riskier products.
- **Red flag:** A price to book of 0.5 times read as a bargain; a high NIM from "unsecured products" (S334); pressure on staff to sell third-party products for fee income; treasury losses when rising rates cut bond values; rising rates squeezing margins (S426).
- **Green flag:** ROE and ROA that justify the price to book; a falling slippage ratio, "very good for a bank" (S334).
- **Where the data is:** Annual report; bank results with provisions, GNPA and NNPA.
- **Thresholds:** RULE (S001): the cash-flow gate is "redundant and void" for banks and NBFCs because their operating cash flow is inherently negative. S426: credit cost is total provisions divided by average total assets. EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S332): Bank of Baroda ROE 18 percent and ROA 1 percent; South Indian Bank at a price to book of 0.61. S359: a bank should have at least double-digit ROE; avoid single-digit ROE banks.
- **Quote:** "A seemingly cheap valuation, such as a 0.5x price-to-book ratio, is often not a value bargain." (S426, TVGPG 00:36:19)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S332, S334, S359, S426)

### 42. Asset quality: GNPA, NNPA, slippage, coverage and book vintage
- **Check:** Read gross and net NPA, the slippage ratio, provision coverage, and whether old and new books differ.
- **Detects:** Credit stress, aggressive lending, and a divergence between reported and true asset quality.
- **Red flag:** Higher credit cost meaning more aggressive lending (S334); an old corporate book with GNPA of 11.9 percent beside a new book at a tiny figure (South Indian Bank, S332); unsecured microfinance, a "deeply cyclical industry" (S333), where aggressive growth is dangerous (S394); true NPAs found by the RBI that diverge from reported ones (S336); "any bad news in banks and you're out" (S336).
- **Green flag:** A granular retail book that spreads risk (S336); a benign phase of net NPAs near 1 percent and shrinking restructured loans (S339).
- **Where the data is:** Bank annual report and quarterly results.
- **Thresholds:** RULE-style definitions: GNPA is "borrowers which haven't paid principal or interest for 90 days or more" (S426); slippage is fresh NPAs as a percentage of standard advances (S334). EXAMPLES, PERISHABLE: provision coverage rose from 58 to 77 percent and net NPA fell from 2.97 to 1.86 (S332); an unsecured card book with NPAs near 2.8 to 2.9 normally and 5 to 5.5 in crises and credit costs of 5.5 to 6 percent (S338); in COVID unsecured SFBs reported GNPAs of about 10.4 to 11.8 percent while secured ones stayed profitable, and Equitas had 53 percent of its book in Tamil Nadu (S333).
- **Quote:** "credit costs are the highest versus other types of lenders in a down cycle" (S339, DECODA 00:26:41)
- **Sources:** 8 (S332, S333, S334, S336, S338, S339, S394, S426)

### 43. NBFC funding, asset-liability mismatch, collateral and microfinance
- **Check:** Match debt maturity to asset maturity, test the cost of funds, and check collateral cover and tenure.
- **Detects:** Mismatch failure, the high-yield trap, and auction losses.
- **Red flag:** Short debt funding long assets (DHFL named, S335, S392); an NBFC that cannot get low-cost funding and pushes high-margin products, which "ends up increasing my risk"; funding that moves to whoever pays a higher return (S387); an RBI loan-to-value cap after strong growth (S388); aggressive microfinance growth, dangerous as the Andhra crisis showed (S394); a transition from NBFC to small finance bank that depresses ROA and ROE (S333); gold loans with a one-year tenure that auction at a loss (S391).
- **Green flag:** Short tenure that sells quickly; discipline on ticket size; the lowest cost of funds.
- **Where the data is:** Annual report and credit-rating disclosure; loan-to-value comes only from there (S003).
- **Thresholds:** RULE (S001 F12, S002): loan-to-value at or below 75 percent, with all four safety conditions. S392: the rate and LTV figures in its lecture are illustrative. EXAMPLE (S397): a 75,000 loan plus 5,000 interest turning NPA recovers 75,000 and forgoes the 5,000 interest.
- **Quote:** "continue to focus on high margin products, which again, ends up increasing my risk" (S335, DECODF 01:57:47)
- **Sources:** 11 (S001, S002, S003, S333, S335, S387, S388, S391, S392, S394, S397)

### 44. Insurers: reserving triangle and float quality
- **Check:** Read the reserving triangle for how conservative or aggressive reserves have been, and the quality of the float.
- **Detects:** Under-provisioning and credit losses in the investment book.
- **Red flag:** Under-provisioning; a company "very aggressive in their accounting" (S378); assumptions changed before an IPO (HDFC Life, S383); exposure to defaulted bonds (IL&FS, DHFL losses booked by insurers, S378, S383).
- **Green flag:** A history of negative deficiency, which "tells about the quality of the books"; a large share of AAA-rated bonds; ICICI Prudential with "zero NPAs in their history of investing" (S383, PERISHABLE).
- **Where the data is:** Annual report reserving triangle and investment note.
- **Thresholds:** The course states no numeric cut. S378: most insurers except one or two "make bulk of the money on your float income rather than underwriting profit". S383: exit unprofitable lines; "nobody is making money in crop".
- **Quote:** "they were never deficient, and that tells about the quality of the books" (S377, UNDERA2 02:12:24)
- **Sources:** 3 (S377, S378, S383)

### 45. Insurers: solvency, persistency, claims ratio and embedded value
- **Check:** Read the solvency ratio, persistency at set milestones, complaints, claims and cost ratios, and product mix.
- **Detects:** Future profit certainty, and metrics that mislead.
- **Red flag:** A low claims ratio that "may not be always good" because genuine claims may be avoided (S380); rising complaints that lead to poor persistency (S380); prolonged low rates hurting guarantee products and a downturn hurting unit-linked ones (S381); an open-architecture policy letting banks sell any insurer's product (S379); embedded-value assumptions that differ by company (S382); only one general-insurance product still tariffed (S384).
- **Green flag:** Higher persistence, which makes future profit more certain; a high cost ratio that is a strategic phase of building an agency network (S380).
- **Where the data is:** Public disclosures of solvency and persistency; annual report.
- **Thresholds:** RULE (S380, set by the regulator): "at least a solvency ratio of 1.5 plus", meaning 150 percent. Persistency is read after the 13th, 49th and 61st month. S382: accounting profit understates economics because of up-front expenses.
- **Quote:** "higher the persistence, more certain your future profits are" (S380, UNDERB2 01:57:18)
- **Sources:** 5 (S379, S380, S381, S382, S384)

## 9. Cyclicals and capital-cycle checks

### 46. Peak earnings and the P/E inversion
- **Check:** For a cyclical, ask where earnings are in the cycle before reading the multiple.
- **Detects:** A cheap-looking P/E on peak earnings and a dear-looking one on depressed earnings.
- **Red flag:** Operating margins at peaks not seen over the previous decade (S031); buying at "peak valuation plus peak margins" (S420); a margin spike that is "short-lived" (S429); high margins that attract competitors, an "open jar of honey" (S075).
- **Green flag:** Studying a cyclical when its P/E looks high from depressed earnings (S305, S063). The traditional P/E looks infinite at the bottom (S446).
- **Where the data is:** Ten-year margin and earnings history; sector price series.
- **Thresholds:** EXAMPLE (S028, S031): at depressed earnings a cyclical can show 60 times; at peak earnings of 30 against price 60 the multiple is 2 times and looks cheap; a low multiple of 4 to 6 reflects unstable earnings. EXAMPLE, PERISHABLE (S432): Polyplex traded at a P/E of 8.7 in 2022 at peak cycle, then profits fell 80 to 90 percent. S005: BOPP and BOPET single-digit P/E was a value trap.
- **Quote:** "PE multiples often appear the highest at the absolute worst point of the market cycle" (S430, TVGPH 00:49:12)
- **Sources:** 11 (S005, S028, S031, S063, S075, S305, S420, S429, S430, S432, S446)

### 47. Capital cycle and supply response
- **Check:** Ask what the high margin will draw in: new capacity, imports, dumping.
- **Detects:** The supply side that ends a boom.
- **Red flag:** No barrier to entry, so "vicious supply-side cycles" (S427); oversupply and commodity pricing in China-linked chemistries (S136); overcapacity that cut an industry to a 4 percent EBITDA margin (S137, PERISHABLE); greenfield transformer capacity landing around FY27 or FY28 (S303); dumping from China (S301, S280).
- **Green flag:** A barrier to entry; the market acts as a "meter of capital cycle" (S087, HOWB 00:18:05).
- **Where the data is:** Capacity announcements, import data, sector margin history.
- **Thresholds:** EXAMPLE (S427): over 100 Chinese solar companies went bankrupt. S302: US generic distributors consolidated from seven to two or three and abnormal profits before 2015 caused capacity additions. S087: model long-term average margins.
- **Quote:** "businesses lacking barriers to entry will inevitably face vicious supply-side cycles" (S427, TVGPA 00:31:01)
- **Sources:** 8 (S087, S136, S137, S280, S301, S302, S303, S427)

### 48. Input spreads, pass-through and fixed-cost leverage
- **Check:** Ask whether input cost moves can be passed on, and how much of the cost base is fixed.
- **Detects:** Margin swings that are mechanical rather than skill.
- **Red flag:** Ignoring input cost in a down cycle; fixed-price contracts built on a raw-material price that then jumps (S301); feedstock exposure ("margin volatility", S363); revenue falling while fixed rent or staff stays (S330); capacity added in a down cycle (S449).
- **Green flag:** Contracts structured so fuel is not the operator's (S214); backward integration to "control your own destiny of raw materials" (S363); fixed-cost leverage that lifts margins when volume returns (S325).
- **Where the data is:** Input price series against output price; fixed-cost share.
- **Thresholds:** RULE (S001 F7): operating leverage kicks in above about 15 to 20 percent growth; S002 (compiled rule set): quarterly sales growth YoY at or above 20. EXAMPLES, PERISHABLE: a maize processor's raw material jumped from "17 to 18 rupees per kilo" to "26 to 27 rupees" and margins collapsed (S301); a 13 percent revenue rise gave 20 percent EBITDA growth at Samhi (S296).
- **Quote:** "margin volatility because your feed stock pricing will be more or less up and down" (S363, FLUORB 00:11:36)
- **Sources:** 9 (S001, S002, S214, S296, S301, S325, S330, S363, S449)

### 49. Policy-dependent and regulated margins
- **Check:** Ask what a change in law, tax or subsidy would do to the margin, and when it could change.
- **Detects:** Moats and demand that disappear by order.
- **Red flag:** "regulatory headwinds are deadly" (S229); a market that exists only because of a subsidy, with "no terminal value" and a policy expiry date (S258); retrospective taxation or a windfall tax (S182, S313); a pricing cap on stents (S353) or on essential medicines (S400); heavy tax on a product, as in alcohol (S221) and tobacco (S177).
- **Green flag:** Demand that survives without the subsidy (S139).
- **Where the data is:** Government notifications and expiry dates; the tax share of the product price.
- **Thresholds:** EXAMPLES, PERISHABLE: "if government subsidies are not available, then you will get 90% down" (S258); about 65 percent of an alcohol bottle's price is taxes, leaving 35 percent as net revenue (S221); a market-coupling change would remove a near 100 percent share (S229).
- **Quote:** "regulatory headwinds are deadly" (S229, IDENTB 00:15:41)
- **Sources:** 9 (S139, S177, S182, S221, S229, S258, S313, S353, S400)

### 50. Value traps, growth traps and euphoria
- **Check:** Separate cheap-because-broken from cheap-because-mispriced, and expensive-because-growing from expensive-because-hyped.
- **Detects:** Flat profit behind a flat chart, penetration stories that are over, and price run-ups without earnings.
- **Red flag:** Flat profit meaning a flat chart ("cheap can get cheaper", S032); a category where "the penetration story is over, only premiumization can be done" (S242); a stock at half book value with repeated one-off damage to ROE (S231); media euphoria and a parabolic price (S111); a large number of IPOs, historically high PE and amateur investors moving to equity at stage 3 tops (S447).
- **Green flag:** Rate of change in earnings, the core of fundamental analysis (S430).
- **Where the data is:** Multi-year profit and chart; sentiment indicators.
- **Thresholds:** RULE-style (S001 F29): growth-trap territory is a P/E of 30 to 50 times plus; S002 (compiled rule set) treats a P/E at or above 30 as an advisory flag only. S001 F38: a P/E of about 60 times needs 40 to 50 percent growth and about 15 to 20 times needs 15 to 20 percent. S001: a price more than 80 percent above the 200-day EMA with a volume spike is a climax signal.
- **Quote:** "Look at HUL's chart over the last six years. You'll see a value trap" (S242, PREMA 00:22:05)
- **Sources:** 8 (S001, S002, S032, S111, S231, S242, S430, S447)

### 51. Sector co-movement and the bad neighbourhood
- **Check:** Ask what the sector is doing before judging one company in it.
- **Detects:** A good company in a sector that is breaking down, and a laggard that moves only in sympathy.
- **Red flag:** A good single stock in a stage 4 sector (S464); buying a sector laggard that moves only in sympathy (S115); lagging in a rally, so it may "get bombed" when the market falls (S121); a sector theme that "hasn't done anything in the last 18 months" (S180).
- **Green flag:** "when a business performs well in conjunction with its sector" (S522, S525); the leading industry group of a bull market (S098).
- **Where the data is:** Sector index, breadth, relative strength.
- **Thresholds:** The course states no co-movement cut. S098: "Sectors always move together and they also break down together".
- **Quote:** "Sectors always move together and they also break down together" (S098, TECHNI 00:16:17)
- **Sources:** 7 (S098, S115, S121, S180, S464, S522, S525)

## 10. Management and disclosure quality

### 52. Guidance against outcome: "walk the talk"
- **Check:** Compare what management said with what happened, over several years, and discount long outlooks.
- **Detects:** Over-promising and drifting targets.
- **Red flag:** Guidance missed, as when a margin that was expected came in at 1.6 percent (S289, PERISHABLE); very long outlooks, "seven year outlooks or nine year outlooks" (S069); guided degrowth (Syngene, S169, PERISHABLE); hiring consultants, which one instructor reads as a red flag (S006); a closed conference call (S283).
- **Green flag:** Guidance that was met; management raising a margin floor from 18 percent to 20 percent and then delivering (S119, PERISHABLE).
- **Where the data is:** Past concall transcripts against later results.
- **Thresholds:** The course states no number. S283: "take everything that the management says with the bucket full of salt". S325 admits: "I don't know if they achieve the guidance or not".
- **Quote:** "whether they are walking the talk or not" (S066, MODULE 00:19:20)
- **Sources:** 8 (S006, S066, S069, S119, S169, S283, S289, S325)

### 53. Disclosure gaps and information quality
- **Check:** Notice what the company stopped saying, and where a sector's information is thin.
- **Detects:** Reduced visibility, a shut plant not told to exchanges, and stories that cannot be checked.
- **Red flag:** Investor presentations stopped (Walchand, S158); "no disclosures beyond 2025" (MTAR, S154); "very poor disclosures" (S417); not disclosing a plant shutdown to exchanges (S068); a minor delay in provident fund deposits as only an amber flag (S068); management stopping interviews (S188); a conference call closed early (S283); flooding that shut a unit not disclosed proactively (S405).
- **Green flag:** Monthly order-book disclosure (S161). Check the environment regulator's website for plant shutdowns (S418).
- **Where the data is:** Exchange filings, DRHP, concalls, annual reports, credit ratings (S512).
- **Thresholds:** The course states no count. S325: "I have not covered this hotel because there is no information on it".
- **Quote:** "they have the capabilities but there are no disclosures beyond 2025 so very tough to say" (S154, NUKE 00:58:15)
- **Sources:** 11 (S068, S154, S158, S161, S188, S283, S325, S405, S417, S418, S512)

### 54. Concentration: customer, product, molecule, plant, input
- **Check:** Ask how much of sales, profit or supply rests on one name.
- **Detects:** A single point of failure.
- **Red flag:** One customer at about 44 percent of revenue (S009, PERISHABLE); leaf spring at 90 percent of revenue (S193); 100 percent of revenue from one customer (Lumax, S239); "Single plant location is also risky" (S160); molecule concentration (S419, S489); brand concentration (S400); a share of revenue in chemicals flagged for a ban (S005, S341); input dependence such as BEMT at BlueJet (S169); commodity-molecule dependence on ibuprofen or paracetamol (S401).
- **Green flag:** A diversified mix, or a concentrated customer who has validated the supplier through advance payment (S245).
- **Where the data is:** Concall segment disclosure; annual report customer note.
- **Thresholds:** No cut. EXAMPLES, PERISHABLE: 40 percent of domestic sales from B2G (S161); coal at 60 percent of a consultancy's revenue (S160); 82 percent of a hospital business from oncology (S350); 10 to 12 percent of one EPC backlog tied to Bangladesh (S256).
- **Quote:** "leaf spring is the bread and butter business of this company because 90% of the revenue comes from leaf springs" (S193, CV 00:42:39)
- **Sources:** 15 (S005, S009, S160, S161, S169, S193, S239, S245, S256, S341, S350, S400, S401, S419, S489)

### 55. Regulatory track record in pharma and chemicals
- **Check:** Look for warning letters, Form 483s, plant shutdowns and clinical binary risk.
- **Detects:** Facility risk that can cut market value in a day.
- **Red flag:** "if there is a warning letter in any company, it's not a good sign" (S148); a repeat history, "a second or third warning letter in the last 7-8 years" (S148); a failed field trial (S207); an NCE trial failure, a "zero or one business" (S302); a "complete lower circuit" on a binary clinical outcome (S251); warning letters hurting the "regulatory track record" (S475).
- **Green flag:** "a solid clean regulatory track record" (S407).
- **Where the data is:** Regulator warning-letter listing; plant-status disclosure; concalls.
- **Thresholds:** RULE-style (S001 F36): any open Form 483, VAI/OAI classification or Warning Letter is a flag. S401: one failed check can cut market cap by 40, 50 or 60 percent. S148: a warning letter on a small share of sales is still a signal.
- **Quote:** "has a solid clean regulatory track record" (S407, APIH 00:03:31)
- **Sources:** 8 (S001, S148, S207, S251, S302, S401, S407, S475)

### 56. Independent verification and the screen hit
- **Check:** Verify a claim from a second source, and treat a screen hit as a start.
- **Detects:** Vendor bias, third-hand claims, hallucinated numbers and "liquidity rigged" names.
- **Red flag:** A supplier describing his own demand (S174); a stock that triggers many screens with no fundamental story, a "liquidity rigged stock" (S001, S462); an AI answering from its own memory (S460); one side's claims in a conflict (S212); capacity claims that do not show in employee cost (S045).
- **Green flag:** Checking management independently (S155); satellite-map plant checks, headcount growth and credit-rating reports (S442); validation in the order book (S207).
- **Where the data is:** Concalls, rating reports, regulator sites, satellite imagery.
- **Thresholds:** The course states no number. S010: use the stricter reading when two inputs differ. S462: for those without conviction, "put your stop loss very closely"; the course does not say a percentage.
- **Quote:** "what they're expecting within the industry is that, again, you can verify this independently, right?" (S189, CV 00:19:37)
- **Sources:** 11 (S001, S010, S155, S174, S189, S207, S212, S442, S460, S462, S045)

### 57. Turnaround screen: look past the P&L loss, then ask why debt was cut
- **Check:** Test whether an accounting loss hides improving EBITDA and cash, and why the balance sheet needed repair.
- **Detects:** A real turnaround against an accounting artifact, and fake scale.
- **Red flag:** Shaky shareholders going in (S293, S297); a private-equity overhang (S295); debt in a cyclical sector (S293); scale funded by debt with weak cash flow (S231); a CFO resigning three times plus an auditor resignation (S116).
- **Green flag:** Stable CFO and senior directors (S298); one-off costs or ESOP expense depressing the reported loss while EBITDA and cash improve (S513, S520, S523, S528); balance-sheet repair by asset sales or capital infusion; a restructuring where the listed entity keeps the profitable business (S113).
- **Where the data is:** EBITDA and cash flow; shareholding pattern; debt schedule.
- **Thresholds:** No cut. S520: a precise model would be false precision in a turnaround, so use a ballpark valuation.
- **Quote:** "the balance sheet can be fixed ahead of, or in parallel with, the P&L recovery" (S527)
- **Sources:** 12 (S113, S116, S231, S293, S295, S297, S513, S520, S523, S527, S528, S298)
